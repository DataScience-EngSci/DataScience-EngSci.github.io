1. The Comparative Experimental Setup & Point Estimation

In empirical engineering, business intelligence, and modern data science, experimental design almost always revolves around comparison rather than isolated estimation:

  • Comparing the mean test scores of students across two distinct school districts.
  • Comparing the conversion rates of two UI layouts in an online A/B testing experiment.
  • Comparing the latency or throughput of two server routing architectures under identical simulated workloads.

Let \(X_1, X_2, \dots, X_{n_1}\) be a random sample of size \(n_1\) drawn from Population 1 with mean \(\mu_1\) and variance \(\sigma_1^2\).
Let \(Y_1, Y_2, \dots, Y_{n_2}\) be an independent random sample of size \(n_2\) drawn from Population 2 with mean \(\mu_2\) and variance \(\sigma_2^2\).

The target parameter is the difference in population means \(\theta = \mu_1 - \mu_2\). Its natural point estimator is the difference between the two sample means: \[ \hat{\theta} = \bar{X} - \bar{Y} = \frac{1}{n_1} \sum_{i=1}^{n_1} X_i - \frac{1}{n_2} \sum_{j=1}^{n_2} Y_j \]

Proposition 1.1 (Expectation and Variance of the Estimator) By linearity of expectation: \[ E[\bar{X} - \bar{Y}] = E[\bar{X}] - E[\bar{Y}] = \mu_1 - \mu_2 \] Hence, \(\bar{X} - \bar{Y}\) is an unbiased estimator of \(\mu_1 - \mu_2\).
Furthermore, since samples 1 and 2 are mutually independent, the covariance between \(\bar{X}\) and \(\bar{Y}\) is zero: \[ \text{Var}(\bar{X} - \bar{Y}) = \text{Var}(\bar{X}) + \text{Var}(\bar{Y}) = \frac{\sigma_1^2}{n_1} + \frac{\sigma_2^2}{n_2} \] The standard error of the estimator is therefore: \[ \sigma_{\bar{X} - \bar{Y}} = \sqrt{\frac{\sigma_1^2}{n_1} + \frac{\sigma_2^2}{n_2}} \]

2. Difference of Means with Known Variances (\(\sigma_1^2, \sigma_2^2\))

If both populations are normally distributed, or if both sample sizes are sufficiently large (\(n_1 \ge 30\) and \(n_2 \ge 30\)) so that the Central Limit Theorem applies, the sampling distribution of \(\bar{X} - \bar{Y}\) is normal: \[ \bar{X} - \bar{Y} \sim \mathcal{N}\left(\mu_1 - \mu_2, \; \frac{\sigma_1^2}{n_1} + \frac{\sigma_2^2}{n_2}\right) \] Standardizing yields the pivotal quantity: \[ Z = \frac{(\bar{X} - \bar{Y}) - (\mu_1 - \mu_2)}{\sqrt{\frac{\sigma_1^2}{n_1} + \frac{\sigma_2^2}{n_2}}} \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1) \]

Theorem 2.1 (Two-Sample Z-Confidence Interval) A \(100(1-\alpha)\%\) confidence interval for \(\mu_1 - \mu_2\) when \(\sigma_1^2\) and \(\sigma_2^2\) are known is given by: \[ (\bar{x}_1 - \bar{x}_2) \pm z_{\alpha/2} \sqrt{\frac{\sigma_1^2}{n_1} + \frac{\sigma_2^2}{n_2}} \] Similarly, the one-sided \(100(1-\alpha)\%\) lower and upper bounds are: \[ \mu_1 - \mu_2 \ge (\bar{x}_1 - \bar{x}_2) - z_\alpha \sqrt{\frac{\sigma_1^2}{n_1} + \frac{\sigma_2^2}{n_2}} \] \[ \mu_1 - \mu_2 \le (\bar{x}_1 - \bar{x}_2) + z_\alpha \sqrt{\frac{\sigma_1^2}{n_1} + \frac{\sigma_2^2}{n_2}} \]
Worked Example: County A vs. County B 5th-Grade Standardized Exam A standardized reading examination is administered to 5th-grade students in two adjacent counties:
  • County A: Sample size \(n_1 = 57\), sample mean \(\bar{x}_1 = 77.2\), known historical variance \(\sigma_1^2 = 15.3\).
  • County B: Sample size \(n_2 = 63\), sample mean \(\bar{x}_2 = 75.3\), known historical variance \(\sigma_2^2 = 19.7\).
We wish to construct a \(99\%\) confidence interval for the difference in mean performance \(\mu_1 - \mu_2\).

Step 1: Point Estimate and Standard Error \[ \bar{x}_1 - \bar{x}_2 = 77.2 - 75.3 = 1.90 \] \[ \text{SE} = \sqrt{\frac{\sigma_1^2}{n_1} + \frac{\sigma_2^2}{n_2}} = \sqrt{\frac{15.3}{57} + \frac{19.7}{63}} = \sqrt{0.26842 + 0.31270} = \sqrt{0.58112} \approx 0.76231 \] Step 2: Critical Value and Margin of Error
For a \(99\%\) confidence level, \(\alpha = 0.01\), so \(\alpha/2 = 0.005\). The standard normal critical value is \(z_{0.005} \approx 2.56\) (or \(2.576\); using the lecture slide convention \(z = 2.56\)): \[ \text{ME} = 2.56 \times 0.76231 \approx 1.9515 \] Step 3: Confidence Interval \[ 1.90 \pm 1.9515 \implies (-0.0515, \; 3.8515) \] Interpretation: Because the interval contains zero (\(-0.0515 < 0 < 3.8515\)), we cannot conclude at the \(99\%\) confidence level that there is a statistically significant difference between the two counties' true mean scores.

3. Methodological Warning: The Hazard of "Data Snooping"

The Danger of "Data Snooping" (Post-Hoc Hypothesis Formulation):
In the previous example, observing \(\bar{x}_1 = 77.2 > \bar{x}_2 = 75.3\), a naive investigator might be tempted to claim: "County A performed better, so let us compute a one-sided 99% lower bound \(\mu_1 - \mu_2 > \bar{x}_1 - \bar{x}_2 - z_{0.01}\text{SE} = 1.90 - 2.33(0.7623) = 1.90 - 1.776 = +0.124 > 0\), proving County A is superior!"

Why this is statistically invalid: If you look at the sample data first to decide which direction or which group is labeled "1" and which is "2", you have performed data snooping. You have selected a one-sided test solely because the observed difference favoured that direction by random chance. Doing so distorts the nominal significance level—the true Type I error rate doubles because you would have made the analogous one-sided claim in the other direction had the sample means been reversed! One-sided hypotheses must be fixed a priori prior to observing data.

4. Difference of Means with Unknown Equal Variances: Pooled \(t\)-Interval

When the population variances \(\sigma_1^2\) and \(\sigma_2^2\) are unknown, and the sample sizes are small (\(n_1, n_2 < 30\)), the Central Limit Theorem cannot be invoked. If we assume that both populations are normally distributed and share an equal underlying variance: \[ \sigma_1^2 = \sigma_2^2 = \sigma^2 \quad \text{(Homoscedasticity)} \] we can combine the information from both samples to form an optimal, unbiased estimator of \(\sigma^2\).

Definition 4.1 (Pooled Sample Variance \(S_p^2\)) The pooled sample variance is the weighted average of the two sample variances \(S_1^2\) and \(S_2^2\), weighted by their respective degrees of freedom: \[ S_p^2 = \frac{(n_1 - 1)S_1^2 + (n_2 - 1)S_2^2}{(n_1 - 1) + (n_2 - 1)} = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n_1}(X_i - \bar{X})^2 + \sum_{j=1}^{n_2}(Y_j - \bar{Y})^2}{n_1 + n_2 - 2} \] The pooled sample standard deviation is \(S_p = \sqrt{S_p^2}\).
Theorem 4.2 (Distribution of the Pooled \(T\)-Statistic) Because the two samples are independent, the sum of independent chi-square random variables has degrees of freedom: \[ \frac{(n_1 + n_2 - 2)S_p^2}{\sigma^2} = \frac{(n_1 - 1)S_1^2}{\sigma^2} + \frac{(n_2 - 1)S_2^2}{\sigma^2} \sim \chi^2(n_1 + n_2 - 2) \] Dividing the standard normal numerator by the independent square root of the chi-square variable over its degrees of freedom gives: \[ T = \frac{(\bar{X} - \bar{Y}) - (\mu_1 - \mu_2)}{S_p \sqrt{\frac{1}{n_1} + \frac{1}{n_2}}} \sim t(n_1 + n_2 - 2) \] The resulting \(100(1-\alpha)\%\) pooled confidence interval is: \[ (\bar{x}_1 - \bar{x}_2) \pm t_{\alpha/2, \; n_1 + n_2 - 2} \cdot S_p \sqrt{\frac{1}{n_1} + \frac{1}{n_2}} \]
Worked Example: Small Sample Comparison with Equal Variances Suppose two independent experiments yield the following summary statistics:
  • Sample 1: \(n_1 = 9\), \(\bar{x}_1 = 23.2\), \(s_1^2 = 4.3\)
  • Sample 2: \(n_2 = 8\), \(\bar{x}_2 = 24.7\), \(s_2^2 = 5.2\)
Construct a \(90\%\) confidence interval for \(\mu_1 - \mu_2\), assuming normal populations with equal variances.

Step 1: Point Estimate and Degrees of Freedom \[ \bar{x}_1 - \bar{x}_2 = 23.2 - 24.7 = -1.5 \] \[ \text{df} = n_1 + n_2 - 2 = 9 + 8 - 2 = 15 \] Step 2: Pooled Variance \[ S_p^2 = \frac{(9 - 1)(4.3) + (8 - 1)(5.2)}{15} = \frac{8(4.3) + 7(5.2)}{15} = \frac{34.4 + 36.4}{15} = \frac{70.8}{15} = 4.72 \] \[ S_p = \sqrt{4.72} \approx 2.17255 \] Step 3: Standard Error \[ \text{SE} = S_p \sqrt{\frac{1}{9} + \frac{1}{8}} = \sqrt{4.72 \left(\frac{1}{9} + \frac{1}{8}\right)} = \sqrt{4.72 \times 0.23611} = \sqrt{1.11444} \approx 1.05567 \] Step 4: Critical Value and Interval
For a \(90\%\) interval, \(\alpha = 0.10 \implies \alpha/2 = 0.05\). From the Student's \(t\)-table with \(\text{df} = 15\): \[ t_{0.05, \; 15} = 1.753 \] \[ \text{ME} = 1.753 \times 1.05567 \approx 1.8506 \] \[ -1.5 \pm 1.8506 \implies (-3.351, \; 0.351) \] Since \(0 \in (-3.351, 0.351)\), there is no statistically significant difference between \(\mu_1\) and \(\mu_2\) at the \(10\%\) significance level.

5. Difference of Means with Unknown Unequal Variances: Welch-Satterthwaite Procedure

When \(\sigma_1^2 \ne \sigma_2^2\), pooling is mathematically invalid and can cause severely distorted confidence levels (especially when sample sizes are unbalanced, \(n_1 \ne n_2\)). This fundamental challenge is known in statistics as the Behrens-Fisher problem.

Under unequal variances, the natural standardized test statistic is: \[ T^* = \frac{(\bar{X} - \bar{Y}) - (\mu_1 - \mu_2)}{\sqrt{\frac{S_1^2}{n_1} + \frac{S_2^2}{n_2}}} \] This statistic does not follow an exact Student's \(t\)-distribution. However, B. L. Welch (1938) and F. E. Satterthwaite (1946) proved that its distribution is exceptionally well approximated by a \(t\)-distribution with effective degrees of freedom \(\nu\).

Theorem 5.1 (Welch-Satterthwaite Degrees of Freedom) The effective degrees of freedom \(\nu\) is given by: \[ \nu = \frac{\left( \frac{S_1^2}{n_1} + \frac{S_2^2}{n_2} \right)^2}{\frac{(S_1^2/n_1)^2}{n_1 - 1} + \frac{(S_2^2/n_2)^2}{n_2 - 1}} \] Because \(\nu\) is generally not an integer, in manual calculations we conservatively round down to the nearest integer: \(\lfloor \nu \rfloor\).
The Welch \(100(1-\alpha)\%\) confidence interval is: \[ (\bar{x}_1 - \bar{x}_2) \pm t_{\alpha/2, \; \lfloor \nu \rfloor} \sqrt{\frac{S_1^2}{n_1} + \frac{S_2^2}{n_2}} \]
Class Assignment 1: Solving the Previous Example via Welch's Method Instructor's handwritten prompt on Slide 8: "Solve the previous example (\(n_1 = 9, \bar{x}_1 = 23.2, s_1^2 = 4.3\) and \(n_2 = 8, \bar{x}_2 = 24.7, s_2^2 = 5.2\)) using Welch's method."

Step 1: Compute Variance Components \[ \frac{s_1^2}{n_1} = \frac{4.3}{9} \approx 0.47778, \quad \frac{s_2^2}{n_2} = \frac{5.2}{8} = 0.65000 \] \[ \frac{s_1^2}{n_1} + \frac{s_2^2}{n_2} = 0.47778 + 0.65000 = 1.12778 \] Step 2: Compute Satterthwaite Degrees of Freedom \[ \text{Numerator} = (1.12778)^2 = 1.27189 \] \[ \text{Denominator} = \frac{(0.47778)^2}{9 - 1} + \frac{(0.65000)^2}{8 - 1} = \frac{0.22827}{8} + \frac{0.42250}{7} = 0.028534 + 0.060357 = 0.088891 \] \[ \nu = \frac{1.27189}{0.088891} \approx 14.308 \implies \lfloor \nu \rfloor = 14 \] Remark: Notice that the Welch degrees of freedom (\(\nu = 14\)) is strictly smaller than the pooled degrees of freedom (\(15\)), penalizing our inference for not assuming equal variances.

Step 3: Construct the 90% Welch Interval
From the \(t\)-table with \(\text{df} = 14\), \(t_{0.05, \; 14} = 1.761\). \[ \text{SE} = \sqrt{1.12778} \approx 1.06197 \] \[ \text{ME} = 1.761 \times 1.06197 \approx 1.8701 \] \[ -1.5 \pm 1.8701 \implies (-3.370, \; 0.370) \] The Welch interval is slightly wider than the pooled interval \((-3.351, 0.351)\), reflecting the additional uncertainty of heteroscedasticity.

6. Computational Implementation in R

In practice, these calculations are executed in statistical computing packages. In R, the function t.test() implements Welch's test by default, and can perform the pooled test by setting var.equal = TRUE: 

# Simulate synthetic sample data
x <- rnorm(10)
y <- rnorm(14)

# 1. Welch Two-Sample t-test (unequal variances, default)
t.test(x, y, conf.level = 0.90)

# 2. Classical Pooled Two-Sample t-test (equal variances assumed)
t.test(x, y, var.equal = TRUE, conf.level = 0.90)

7. Confidence Intervals for a Single Population Proportion

Let \(X\) denote the number of successes in \(n\) independent Bernoulli trials with unknown probability \(p\), so \(X \sim \text{Binomial}(n, p)\). The sample proportion is: \[ \hat{p} = \frac{X}{n} \] We know that \(E[\hat{p}] = p\) and \(\text{Var}(\hat{p}) = \frac{p(1-p)}{n}\). By the Central Limit Theorem: \[ Z = \frac{\hat{p} - p}{\sqrt{\frac{p(1-p)}{n}}} \xrightarrow{d} \mathcal{N}(0, 1) \]

The "3-Sigma" Normality Check:
To ensure the normal approximation to the binomial distribution is valid, the distribution must not have probability mass truncated at 0 or 1. The slide requirement specifies the 3-sigma rule: \[ \left[ \hat{p} - 3\sqrt{\frac{\hat{p}(1-\hat{p})}{n}}, \; \hat{p} + 3\sqrt{\frac{\hat{p}(1-\hat{p})}{n}} \right] \subset [0, 1] \]
Theorem 7.1 (Exact Quadratic vs. Standard Wald Interval) The probability condition \(P(-z_{\alpha/2} < Z < z_{\alpha/2}) = 1 - \alpha\) leads to the inequality: \[ \left( \frac{\hat{p} - p}{\sqrt{\frac{p(1-p)}{n}}} \right)^2 \le z_{\alpha/2}^2 \iff (\hat{p} - p)^2 \le z_{\alpha/2}^2 \frac{p(1-p)}{n} \] Expanding and rearranging as a quadratic in \(p\): \[ p^2 \left( 1 + \frac{z_{\alpha/2}^2}{n} \right) - p \left( 2\hat{p} + \frac{z_{\alpha/2}^2}{n} \right) + \hat{p}^2 \le 0 \] Solving this quadratic yields the Wilson Score Interval.

If instead we substitute the sample estimate \(\hat{p}\) directly into the standard error denominator (the Wald approximation), we obtain the standard formula: \[ \hat{p} \pm z_{\alpha/2} \sqrt{\frac{\hat{p}(1-\hat{p})}{n}} \]
Worked Example: Political Mayoral Poll In an election survey, \(n = 500\) randomly selected voters are interviewed, and \(x = 320\) state they support Candidate A.

Step 1: Point Estimate and 3-Sigma Check \[ \hat{p} = \frac{320}{500} = \frac{16}{25} = 0.64 \] \[ \text{SE} = \sqrt{\frac{0.64(1 - 0.64)}{500}} = \sqrt{\frac{0.64 \times 0.36}{500}} = \sqrt{\frac{0.2304}{500}} = \sqrt{0.0004608} \approx 0.021466 \] Check: \(\hat{p} \pm 3 \times \text{SE} = 0.64 \pm 3(0.021466) = 0.64 \pm 0.06440 = [0.5756, \; 0.7044] \subset [0, 1]\). The approximation is valid.

Step 2: 95% Confidence Interval Calculation
With \(\alpha = 0.05\), \(z_{0.025} = 1.96\): \[ \text{ME} = 1.96 \times 0.021466 \approx 0.04207 \] \[ 0.64 \pm 0.04207 \implies (0.5979, \; 0.6821) \] We are \(95\%\) confident that the candidate's true support in the electorate lies between \(59.79\%\) and \(68.21\%\).

8. Sample Size Determination for Proportions

Suppose an investigator desires a specified margin of error \(E\) with confidence \(1 - \alpha\): \[ E = z_{\alpha/2} \sqrt{\frac{p(1-p)}{n}} \implies n = \frac{z_{\alpha/2}^2 \cdot p(1-p)}{E^2} \] Since \(p\) is unknown before the survey is conducted, we observe that the parabolic function \(g(p) = p(1-p)\) achieves its absolute maximum at \(p = 0.5\), where \(p(1-p) = 0.25 = \frac{1}{4}\).

Proposition 8.1 (Conservative Sample Size Formula) To guarantee that the margin of error does not exceed \(E\), regardless of the true value of \(p\), choose: \[ n \ge \left\lceil \frac{z_{\alpha/2}^2}{4 E^2} \right\rceil \] For example, for a \(95\%\) confidence level (\(z_{0.025} = 1.96\)) and a \(3\%\) margin of error (\(E = 0.03\)): \[ n \ge \frac{(1.96)^2}{4(0.03)^2} = \frac{3.8416}{0.0036} \approx 1067.11 \implies n = 1068 \]

9. Difference of Two Independent Proportions & A/B Testing

Let \(X_1 \sim \text{Binomial}(n_1, p_1)\) and \(X_2 \sim \text{Binomial}(n_2, p_2)\) be two independent samples with sample proportions \(\hat{p}_1 = X_1/n_1\) and \(\hat{p}_2 = X_2/n_2\).

Theorem 9.1 (Confidence Interval for \(p_1 - p_2\)) By independence: \[ E[\hat{p}_1 - \hat{p}_2] = p_1 - p_2, \quad \text{Var}(\hat{p}_1 - \hat{p}_2) = \frac{p_1(1-p_1)}{n_1} + \frac{p_2(1-p_2)}{n_2} \] The large-sample \(100(1-\alpha)\%\) confidence interval is: \[ (\hat{p}_1 - \hat{p}_2) \pm z_{\alpha/2} \sqrt{\frac{\hat{p}_1(1-\hat{p}_1)}{n_1} + \frac{\hat{p}_2(1-\hat{p}_2)}{n_2}} \]
Worked Example: Comparing Candidate Support in Two Geographic Regions A campaign conducts polling in two regions:
  • Region 1: Sample size \(n_1 = 500\), supporters \(x_1 = 320 \implies \hat{p}_1 = \frac{320}{500} = 0.6400\)
  • Region 2: Sample size \(n_2 = 420\), supporters \(x_2 = 302 \implies \hat{p}_2 = \frac{302}{420} = \frac{151}{210} \approx 0.7190\)
Construct a \(90\%\) confidence interval for the difference \(p_1 - p_2\).

Step 1: Point Estimate and Variance Components \[ \hat{p}_1 - \hat{p}_2 = 0.6400 - 0.7190 = -0.0790 \] \[ \frac{\hat{p}_1(1-\hat{p}_1)}{n_1} = \frac{0.64 \times 0.36}{500} = \frac{0.2304}{500} = 0.0004608 \] \[ \frac{\hat{p}_2(1-\hat{p}_2)}{n_2} = \frac{0.7190 \times 0.2810}{420} = \frac{0.20204}{420} \approx 0.0004810 \] \[ \text{SE} = \sqrt{0.0004608 + 0.0004810} = \sqrt{0.0009418} \approx 0.03069 \] Step 2: 90% Confidence Interval
For \(90\%\) confidence, \(\alpha = 0.10 \implies z_{0.05} = 1.645\). \[ \text{ME} = 1.645 \times 0.03069 \approx 0.05048 \] \[ -0.0790 \pm 0.0505 \implies (-0.1295, \; -0.0285) \] Conclusion: Since the upper bound \(-0.0285 < 0\), the entire \(90\%\) confidence interval lies strictly below zero. We conclude that Candidate A has a statistically significantly higher proportion of support in Region 2 than in Region 1.

10. Confidence Intervals for Population Variance and Standard Deviation (\(\chi^2\))

In quality engineering and reliability analysis, estimating the spread or variability is as critical as estimating the center. Let \(X_1, \dots, X_n\) be a random sample of size \(n\) from a normal population \(\mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2)\).

Theorem 10.1 (Pivotal Quantity for Variance) The pivotal quantity: \[ Q = \frac{(n-1)S^2}{\sigma^2} \sim \chi^2(n-1) \] Because the Chi-Square distribution is asymmetric (skewed right with support on \([0, \infty)\)), critical values \(\chi_{1-\alpha/2, n-1}^2\) and \(\chi_{\alpha/2, n-1}^2\) are determined separately: \[ P\left( \chi_{1-\alpha/2, \; n-1}^2 < \frac{(n-1)S^2}{\sigma^2} < \chi_{\alpha/2, \; n-1}^2 \right) = 1 - \alpha \] Inverting the inequality yields the exact \(100(1-\alpha)\%\) two-sided confidence interval for \(\sigma^2\): \[ \frac{(n-1)S^2}{\chi_{\alpha/2, \; n-1}^2} < \sigma^2 < \frac{(n-1)S^2}{\chi_{1-\alpha/2, \; n-1}^2} \] Taking square roots gives the confidence interval for the population standard deviation \(\sigma\): \[ \sqrt{\frac{(n-1)S^2}{\chi_{\alpha/2, \; n-1}^2}} < \sigma < \sqrt{\frac{(n-1)S^2}{\chi_{1-\alpha/2, \; n-1}^2}} \]
Worked Example: Potato Chip Fill Weight Variability A quality control manager at a snack food packaging facility collects a random sample of \(n = 20\) bags of potato chips and finds a sample variance of \(s^2 = 0.51 \text{ oz}^2\). Assuming that bag fill weights follow a normal distribution, find \(95\%\) confidence intervals for \(\sigma\).

Here, \(n - 1 = 19\) degrees of freedom, and \((n-1)s^2 = 19 \times 0.51 = 9.69\).

1. Two-Sided 95% Confidence Interval:
From the \(\chi^2\)-distribution with \(\text{df} = 19\): \[ \chi_{0.975, \; 19}^2 = 8.90652, \quad \chi_{0.025, \; 19}^2 = 32.85233 \] Setting up the inequality: \[ 8.90652 < \frac{(20 - 1)(0.51)}{\sigma^2} < 32.85233 \] \[ 0.91915 < \frac{1}{\sigma^2} < 3.39033 \implies 0.29496 < \sigma^2 < 1.08797 \] Taking square roots: \[ 0.54310 < \sigma < 1.04306 \]
2. One-Sided Bounds (Slide 14):
  • Lower-Bounded 95% Interval: Using \(\chi_{0.05, 19}^2 = 30.14353\): \[ 0 < \frac{19(0.51)}{\sigma^2} < 30.14353 \implies \sigma^2 > \frac{9.69}{30.14353} = 0.32146 \implies \mathbf{0.56698 < \sigma < \infty} \]
  • Upper-Bounded 95% Interval: Using \(\chi_{0.95, 19}^2 = 10.11701\): \[ 10.11701 < \frac{19(0.51)}{\sigma^2} < \infty \implies \sigma^2 < \frac{9.69}{10.11701} = 0.95779 \implies \mathbf{0 < \sigma < 0.97867} \] Pedagogical note on slide typo: On Slide 14, the slide formula displays \((19-1)(0.51)\) in the numerator of this step, but calculates the numerical output as \(\sqrt{9.69 / 10.11701} = 0.97867\), confirming that the correct numerator \((20-1)s^2 = 19 \times 0.51 = 9.69\) was used.
