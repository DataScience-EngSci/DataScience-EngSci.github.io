1. Computational Identities of Fisher Information

Evaluating Fisher Information directly from its original definition: \[ I_n(\theta) = \text{Var}\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}\ln f(\mathbf{X}; \theta)\right) = E\left[\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}\ln f(\mathbf{X}; \theta)\right)^2\right] \] frequently requires calculating difficult multidimensional integrals involving squared derivatives. Fortunately, three foundational identities dramatically simplify this computation.

Theorem 1.1 (The Three Canonical Fisher Identities) Under standard regularity conditions (differentiability under the integral sign and support independent of \(\theta\)):
  1. First Bartlett Identity (Zero Expected Score): \[ E\left[\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}\ln f(X; \theta)\right] = 0 \]
  2. Second Bartlett Identity (Second-Derivative Identity): \[ I_1(\theta) = -E\left[\frac{\partial^2}{\partial\theta^2}\ln f(X; \theta)\right] \]
  3. Additivity under Independence: If \(X_1, \dots, X_n\) are independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.), then: \[ I_n(\theta) = n I_1(\theta) \]

2. Proof of the Second-Derivative Identity (Second Bartlett Identity)

Proof of the Second Bartlett Identity Start from the total probability integral condition: \[ \int f(x; \theta) \, dx = 1 \] Differentiating both sides with respect to \(\theta\) yields the first identity: \[ \frac{\partial}{\partial\theta} \int f(x; \theta) \, dx = \int \frac{\partial f(x; \theta)}{\partial\theta} \, dx = \int \left(\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}\ln f(x; \theta)\right) f(x; \theta) \, dx = E\left[\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}\ln f(X; \theta)\right] = 0 \] Now, differentiate both sides of this expectation integral once more with respect to \(\theta\): \[ \frac{\partial}{\partial\theta} \int \left(\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}\ln f(x; \theta)\right) f(x; \theta) \, dx = 0 \] Applying the product rule under the integral sign: \[ \int \left[ \left(\frac{\partial^2}{\partial\theta^2}\ln f(x; \theta)\right) f(x; \theta) + \left(\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}\ln f(x; \theta)\right) \left(\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta} f(x; \theta)\right) \right] dx = 0 \] Recall from calculus that \(\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}f(x; \theta) = f(x; \theta) \frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}\ln f(x; \theta)\). Substituting this into the second term: \[ \int \left(\frac{\partial^2}{\partial\theta^2}\ln f(x; \theta)\right) f(x; \theta) \, dx + \int \left(\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}\ln f(x; \theta)\right)^2 f(x; \theta) \, dx = 0 \] Converting both integrals to expectations: \[ E\left[\frac{\partial^2}{\partial\theta^2}\ln f(X; \theta)\right] + E\left[\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}\ln f(X; \theta)\right)^2\right] = 0 \] Rearranging terms establishes the result: \[ I_1(\theta) = E\left[\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}\ln f(X; \theta)\right)^2\right] = -E\left[\frac{\partial^2}{\partial\theta^2}\ln f(X; \theta)\right] \quad \blacksquare \]

3. Additivity of Fisher Information for i.i.d. Samples

The slides present two distinct, highly illuminating proofs that \(I_n(\theta) = n I_1(\theta)\).

Proof Method 1: Via Score Summation and Independence (Slide 8.1) For i.i.d. samples \(\mathbf{X} = (X_1, \dots, X_n)\), the joint log-likelihood is: \[ \ln f(\mathbf{X}; \theta) = \sum_{i=1}^n \ln f(X_i; \theta) \implies \frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}\ln f(\mathbf{X}; \theta) = \sum_{i=1}^n \frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}\ln f(X_i; \theta) \] Squaring this sum expands into diagonal and off-diagonal cross terms: \[ I_n(\theta) = E\left[\left(\sum_{i=1}^n \frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}\ln f(X_i; \theta)\right)^2\right] = \sum_{i=1}^n \sum_{j=1}^n E\left[ \left(\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}\ln f(X_i; \theta)\right) \left(\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}\ln f(X_j; \theta)\right) \right] \] We evaluate the expectation in two cases:
  • Diagonal terms (\(i = j\)): \[ E\left[\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}\ln f(X_i; \theta)\right)^2\right] = I_1(\theta) \] There are \(n\) such diagonal terms.
  • Off-diagonal terms (\(i \neq j\)): By mutual independence of \(X_i\) and \(X_j\), the expectation factors into the product of expectations: \[ E\left[ \left(\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}\ln f(X_i; \theta)\right) \left(\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}\ln f(X_j; \theta)\right) \right] = E\left[\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}\ln f(X_i; \theta)\right] \cdot E\left[\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}\ln f(X_j; \theta)\right] = 0 \cdot 0 = 0 \] by the First Bartlett Identity!
Summing all \(n^2\) terms: \[ I_n(\theta) = \sum_{i=1}^n I_1(\theta) + \sum_{i \neq j} 0 = n I_1(\theta) \quad \blacksquare \]
Proof Method 2: Via Linearity of Second Derivatives Differentiating the joint log-likelihood twice: \[ \frac{\partial^2}{\partial\theta^2}\ln f(\mathbf{X}; \theta) = \sum_{i=1}^n \frac{\partial^2}{\partial\theta^2}\ln f(X_i; \theta) \] Taking expectations on both sides and applying linearity: \[ I_n(\theta) = -E\left[\sum_{i=1}^n \frac{\partial^2}{\partial\theta^2}\ln f(X_i; \theta)\right] = \sum_{i=1}^n \left(-E\left[\frac{\partial^2}{\partial\theta^2}\ln f(X_i; \theta)\right]\right) = \sum_{i=1}^n I_1(\theta) = n I_1(\theta) \quad \blacksquare \]

4. Detailed Application: Exponential Distribution CRLB

Example 4.1 (CRLB for Exponential Parameters) Let \(X_1, \dots, X_n \overset{\text{i.i.d.}}{\sim} \text{Exponential}(\lambda)\), with PDF \(f(x; \lambda) = \lambda e^{-\lambda x}\) for \(x > 0\).

Step 1: Compute single-observation Fisher Information via Second Derivative:

\[ \ln f(x; \lambda) = \ln \lambda - \lambda x \implies \frac{\partial}{\partial\lambda}\ln f = \frac{1}{\lambda} - x \implies \frac{\partial^2}{\partial\lambda^2}\ln f = -\frac{1}{\lambda^2} \] Notice that \(\frac{\partial^2}{\partial\lambda^2}\ln f\) is constant with respect to \(x\). Therefore: \[ I_1(\lambda) = -E\left[-\frac{1}{\lambda^2}\right] = \frac{1}{\lambda^2} \] Step 2: Total Fisher Information for sample size \(n\): \[ I_n(\lambda) = n I_1(\lambda) = \frac{n}{\lambda^2} \] Step 3: CRLB for estimating \(\tau(\lambda) = \lambda\) directly: Since \(\tau'(\lambda) = 1\): \[ \text{CRLB}(\lambda) = \frac{1}{I_n(\lambda)} = \frac{\lambda^2}{n} \] Step 4: CRLB for estimating the survival/reliability function \(\tau(\lambda) = e^{-\lambda}\) (reliability at \(t = 1\)): \[ \tau'(\lambda) = \frac{d}{d\lambda}e^{-\lambda} = -e^{-\lambda} \implies [\tau'(\lambda)]^2 = e^{-2\lambda} \] \[ \text{CRLB}(\tau(\lambda)) = \frac{[\tau'(\lambda)]^2}{I_n(\lambda)} = \frac{e^{-2\lambda}}{n/\lambda^2} = \frac{\lambda^2 e^{-2\lambda}}{n} \]

5. From Deterministic Calculus Limits to Random Variable Sequences

In classical single-variable calculus, we study the convergence of deterministic sequences of real numbers \(\{a_n\}_{n=1}^\infty\). For example: \[ \lim_{n \to \infty} \frac{1}{n} = 0, \quad \lim_{n \to \infty} \frac{2n^2 + 3}{3n^3 - 7} = 0, \quad \lim_{n \to \infty} \frac{2n^2 + 3}{3n^2 - 7} = \frac{2}{3}, \quad \lim_{n \to \infty} \frac{n - 1}{n} = 1 \] The formal definition states that \(a_n \to L\) if for every \(\varepsilon > 0\), there exists an integer \(N\) such that \(|a_n - L| < \varepsilon\) for all \(n \ge N\).

However, in data science and statistics, we work with a sequence of random variables \(X_1, X_2, \dots, X_n\) (such as sample means \(\bar{X}_n\)). What does it mean to ask: \[ \lim_{n \to \infty} X_n = ? \] Each \(X_n\) is not a single number, but a function defined on a sample space \(\Omega \to \mathbb{R}\). For any finite \(n\), \(X_n\) produces random values that fluctuate from experiment to experiment. We cannot simply require \(|X_n - X| < \varepsilon\) deterministically for all outcomes.

Instead, we assess convergence through the lens of probability: we require that the total probability mass of outcomes where \(X_n\) deviates from its target by more than \(\varepsilon\) shrinks to zero as \(n\) approaches infinity.

6. Concentration Inequalities: Generalized Markov & Chebyshev

To prove that probabilities of deviations vanish, we need upper bounds on tail probabilities that require minimal assumptions about the underlying distribution.

Theorem 6.1 (Generalized Markov's Inequality) Let \(X\) be any random variable, and let \(g(x) \ge 0\) be a non-negative function. Then for any positive constant \(c > 0\): \[ P(g(X) \ge c) \le \frac{E[g(X)]}{c} \]
Proof of Generalized Markov's Inequality The expected value \(E[g(X)]\) can be written as an integral over the real line partitioned into two disjoint sets, \(\{x: g(x) \ge c\}\) and \(\{x: g(x) < c\}\): \[ E[g(X)] = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} g(x) f_X(x) \, dx = \int_{\{x: g(x) \ge c\}} g(x) f_X(x) \, dx + \int_{\{x: g(x) < c\}} g(x) f_X(x) \, dx \] Since \(g(x) \ge 0\) and \(f_X(x) \ge 0\), the second integral is non-negative: \[ \int_{\{x: g(x) < c\}} g(x) f_X(x) \, dx \ge 0 \] Moreover, on the set \(\{x: g(x) \ge c\}\), the function satisfies \(g(x) \ge c\). Therefore: \[ E[g(X)] \ge \int_{\{x: g(x) \ge c\}} g(x) f_X(x) \, dx \ge \int_{\{x: g(x) \ge c\}} c f_X(x) \, dx = c \int_{\{x: g(x) \ge c\}} f_X(x) \, dx \] The remaining integral is exactly the probability of the event \(\{g(X) \ge c\}\): \[ E[g(X)] \ge c \cdot P(g(X) \ge c) \implies P(g(X) \ge c) \le \frac{E[g(X)]}{c} \quad \blacksquare \]
Theorem 6.2 (Chebyshev's Inequality) Let \(X\) be any random variable with mean \(\mu = E[X]\) and finite variance \(\sigma^2 = \text{Var}(X) < \infty\). Then for any \(\varepsilon > 0\): \[ P(|X - \mu| \ge \varepsilon) \le \frac{\sigma^2}{\varepsilon^2} \] Equivalently, setting \(\varepsilon = k\sigma\) for \(k > 0\): \[ P(|X - \mu| \ge k\sigma) \le \frac{1}{k^2} \]
Proof of Chebyshev's Inequality Observe that the event \(\{|X - \mu| \ge \varepsilon\}\) is algebraically identical to \(\{(X - \mu)^2 \ge \varepsilon^2\}\). Choose the non-negative function \(g(x) = (x - \mu)^2\) and constant \(c = \varepsilon^2\) in Markov's Inequality: \[ P(|X - \mu| \ge \varepsilon) = P\left((X - \mu)^2 \ge \varepsilon^2\right) \le \frac{E[(X - \mu)^2]}{\varepsilon^2} = \frac{\text{Var}(X)}{\varepsilon^2} = \frac{\sigma^2}{\varepsilon^2} \] Substituting \(\varepsilon = k\sigma\) yields: \[ P(|X - \mu| \ge k\sigma) \le \frac{\sigma^2}{(k\sigma)^2} = \frac{1}{k^2} \quad \blacksquare \]

7. Convergence in Probability & The Weak Law of Large Numbers (WLLN)

Definition 7.1 (Convergence in Probability) A sequence of random variables \(X_1, X_2, \dots\) converges in probability to a random variable \(X\) (written \(X_n \overset{P}{\longrightarrow} X\)), if for every \(\varepsilon > 0\): \[ \lim_{n \to \infty} P(|X_n - X| \ge \varepsilon) = 0 \quad \left(\text{or equivalently, } \lim_{n \to \infty} P(|X_n - X| < \varepsilon) = 1\right) \] Note: The target \(X\) can be a degenerate random variable that equals a deterministic constant \(c\) with probability 1 (i.e., \(P(X = c) = 1\)), in which case we write \(X_n \overset{P}{\longrightarrow} c\).
Theorem 7.2 (The Weak Law of Large Numbers - WLLN) Let \(X_1, X_2, \dots, X_n\) be independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) random variables with finite expectation \(E[X_i] = \mu\) and finite variance \(\text{Var}(X_i) = \sigma^2 < \infty\). Then the sample average \(\bar{X}_n = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n X_i\) converges in probability to the population mean \(\mu\): \[ \bar{X}_n \overset{P}{\longrightarrow} \mu \quad \text{as } n \to \infty \]
Proof of WLLN (Slide 8.2) Recall the fundamental properties of the sample mean: \[ E[\bar{X}_n] = \mu, \quad \text{Var}(\bar{X}_n) = \sigma_{\bar{X}}^2 = \frac{\sigma^2}{n} \implies \sigma_{\bar{X}} = \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}} \] Apply Chebyshev's inequality to the random variable \(\bar{X}_n\) with tolerance \(\varepsilon > 0\): \[ P(|\bar{X}_n - \mu| \ge \varepsilon) \le \frac{\text{Var}(\bar{X}_n)}{\varepsilon^2} = \frac{\sigma^2}{n \varepsilon^2} \] Alternatively, using the standard deviation formulation with \(k \sigma_{\bar{X}} = \varepsilon \implies k = \frac{\varepsilon\sqrt{n}}{\sigma}\): \[ P(|\bar{X}_n - \mu| \ge \varepsilon) = P\left(|\bar{X}_n - \mu| \ge \left(\frac{\varepsilon\sqrt{n}}{\sigma}\right)\sigma_{\bar{X}}\right) \le \frac{1}{k^2} = \frac{\sigma^2}{n \varepsilon^2} \] Taking the limit as \(n \to \infty\) for any fixed, arbitrary \(\varepsilon > 0\): \[ 0 \le \lim_{n \to \infty} P(|\bar{X}_n - \mu| \ge \varepsilon) \le \lim_{n \to \infty} \frac{\sigma^2}{n \varepsilon^2} = 0 \] By the squeeze theorem: \[ \lim_{n \to \infty} P(|\bar{X}_n - \mu| \ge \varepsilon) = 0 \implies \bar{X}_n \overset{P}{\longrightarrow} \mu \quad \blacksquare \]

8. Applications: Empirical Averages of Exponential and Gamma

Example 8.1 (Sample Mean of Exponential Distribution) Suppose \(X_1, \dots, X_n \overset{\text{i.i.d.}}{\sim} \text{Exponential}(\lambda)\). The population moments are: \[ E[X_i] = \frac{1}{\lambda}, \quad \text{Var}(X_i) = \frac{1}{\lambda^2} < \infty \] Since both moments are finite, Theorem 7.2 immediately applies: \[ \bar{X}_n = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n X_i \overset{P}{\longrightarrow} \frac{1}{\lambda} \] The empirical average converges in probability to the theoretical mean life time \(1/\lambda\).
Example 8.2 (Sample Mean of Gamma Distribution) Suppose \(X_1, \dots, X_n \overset{\text{i.i.d.}}{\sim} \text{Gamma}(\alpha, \beta)\) with PDF \(f(x) = \frac{\beta^\alpha}{\Gamma(\alpha)} x^{\alpha - 1} e^{-\beta x}\) for \(x > 0\). The population mean and variance are: \[ E[X_i] = \frac{\alpha}{\beta}, \quad \text{Var}(X_i) = \frac{\alpha}{\beta^2} < \infty \] Applying the Weak Law of Large Numbers: \[ \bar{X}_n = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n X_i \overset{P}{\longrightarrow} \frac{\alpha}{\beta} \] This guarantees that no matter how complex the sampling variability, empirical averages of gamma variables will concentrate tightly around \(\alpha/\beta\) as \(n\) grows large.
