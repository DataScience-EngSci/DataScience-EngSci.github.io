1. What is Data Science? The Convergence of Skills

At its core, data science is using data to answer questions. Because questions arise in virtually every domain of science, engineering, commerce, and public policy, data science is inherently a multifaceted, interdisciplinary discipline combining:

  • Mathematics, statistics, and machine learning theory
  • Computer science, algorithms, and software engineering
  • Data wrangling, cleaning, structuring, and formatting
  • Exploratory data analysis, visualization, and domain storytelling
The Economist Definition of a Data Scientist

An Economist Special Report captured this unique synthesis of competencies:

“A data scientist is someone who combines the skills of software programmer, statistician, and storyteller slash artist to extract the nuggets of gold hidden under mountains of data.”

2. The Impetus: Massive Data & Inexpensive Compute

Why has data science experienced such explosive growth over the past decade? The discipline arose from the convergence of two independent technological revolutions:

  1. Explosion of Empirical Data: Sensors, mobile devices, transactional engines, genomics platforms, and internet logs generate vast, unceasing streams of observational data across every human endeavor.
  2. Rise of Inexpensive Compute & Storage: Commodity server clusters, high-capacity memory, modern GPUs, distributed compute frameworks, and cloud architectures made storing and processing petabytes economically feasible.

This “perfect storm” provides both the raw material (rich datasets) and the machinery (computational tools), creating an urgent scientific need for analysts skilled at translating raw bits into actionable decisions.

3. Big Data: IBM's Four Vs Framework

The slide deck characterizes the operational challenges of big data through IBM's classic Four Vs taxonomy:

The 4Vs of Big Data
  1. Volume: The sheer physical scale of data generated and captured, routinely measured in gigabytes, terabytes, petabytes, and exabytes.
  2. Variety: The structural diversity of incoming information. Modern datasets mix structured relational tables with unstructured text, environmental telemetry, transactional feeds, social networks, and multimedia audio/video.
  3. Velocity: The speed at which data is in motion—the frequency and latency requirements of data generation, ingestion, and real-time inference.
  4. Veracity: The degree of uncertainty, noise, corruption, and incompleteness inherent in empirical data. Veracity demands that a data scientist evaluate: “Is this data sufficiently reliable and accurate to support high-stakes decision-making?”

4. Five Key Skills of Successful Data Scientists

To execute end-to-end data initiatives, practitioners require five complementary skillsets:

The Professional Skill Spectrum
  1. Business & Domain Expertise: Articulating how insights create tangible value across an organization's value chain (marketing, sales, distribution, pricing, risk, finance) and identifying which questions truly matter. Often, analytics experts pair directly with Subject Matter Experts (SMEs).
  2. Machine Learning Algorithms: Identifying the appropriate technique (predictive modeling, clustering, optimization) for a given problem class, tuning hyperparameters, and translating mathematical results into actionable insights using tools like Python, R, SPSS, or KNIME.
  3. Software Engineering: Developing modular, reproducible computational pipelines, modifying existing libraries, and architecting solutions deployable on-premises or in the cloud.
  4. Data Storage & Data Engineering: Mastering relational (SQL) and non-relational (NoSQL) databases, ETL pipelines, web scraping, and distributed big-data stores like Hadoop, Spark, MongoDB, and Elasticsearch.
  5. Visual Presentation & Communication: Translating analytical findings into intuitive visual interfaces (dashboards, charts, animations) that allow non-technical executives to embrace data-driven recommendations.

5. The End-to-End Data Science Lifecycle

Data science projects follow a structured empirical lifecycle:

Phase 1: Business Understanding & Problem Definition

Every initiative begins by converting high-level business goals into precise, targetable analytical questions. Questions typically fall into standard archetypes: Comparison, Description, Regression, Classification, Clustering, Anomaly Detection, or Recommendation.

Slide Case Study: Translating Business Goals (Netflix Example)

Commercial organizations focus primarily on business performance rather than raw algorithmic loss functions. Consider a streaming platform like Netflix:

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{\textbf{Business Objective:}} & \quad \text{Increase corporate revenue by } 5\% \\ \Downarrow & \\ \text{\textbf{Operational Lever:}} & \quad \text{Increase customer retention rate by } 8\% \\ \Downarrow & \\ \text{\textbf{Machine Learning Task:}} & \quad \text{Increase recommendation engine prediction accuracy by } 10\% \end{aligned} \]

Metrics must always be SMART: Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Relevant, and Time-bound.

Phase 2: Data Collection

Systematically gathering information: identifying what data is required, where it resides, how to query or scrape it, and establishing storage schemas that support high-throughput retrieval.

Phase 3: Data Cleaning & Preprocessing

Empirical datasets are noisy and uncurated. Cleaning addresses duplicates, irrelevant attributes, formatting errors, missing entries, and extreme outliers.

Standardization vs. Normalization
  • Standardization: Enforcing a uniform format across all entries.
    Slide Example: Reformatting heterogeneous phone number entries (e.g. +989121234567, 00989121234567, 09121234567, 9121234567) into a single canonical international standard.
  • Normalization: Rescaling numerical values into a common geometric scale (e.g., Min-Max normalization mapping values into \([0, 1]\), or \(z\)-score standardization to zero mean and unit variance).

Phase 4: Feature Engineering

Using domain knowledge to transform raw measurements into informative predictor variables.

  • Feature Selection: Removing redundant or noisy features via:
    • Filter Methods: Ranking features by statistical metrics (correlation, mutual information) independently of model training.
    • Wrapper Methods: Treating selection as a combinatorial search problem (forward stepwise, backward elimination) evaluated by model cross-validation.
    • Embedded Methods: Performing selection directly inside model optimization (e.g. Lasso \(\ell_1\) regularization).
  • Feature Construction: Creating novel composite features (ratios, interaction terms, temporal aggregations) that expose the underlying physical structure.

Phase 5 & 6: Predictive Modeling, Evaluation & Deployment

Training supervised or unsupervised models, tuning hyperparameters, benchmarking on validation sets, and exposing models to stakeholders via dashboards and real-time APIs.

6. Taxonomy of Data: Types & Measurement Scales

The lecture formalizes the definition of data based on authoritative sources:

Formal Definitions of Data
  • Cambridge English Dictionary: “Information, especially facts or numbers, collected to be examined and considered and used to help decision-making.”
  • Wikipedia: “A set of values of qualitative or quantitative variables.”

Here, the set corresponds to the underlying statistical population, while the variables are the measured attributes.

Hierarchical Taxonomy of Data Types
Superclass Category Mathematical Nature Slide Examples
Categorical (Qualitative) Nominal Unordered categories; no inherent ranking. Gender, blood group, country of origin.
Ordinal Ordered categories with meaningful sequence; intervals between ranks are non-uniform. Academic grading sequence (A, B, C), customer satisfaction tiers.
Binary Special categorical case with exactly two mutually exclusive states. Pass/Fail, Yes/No, Defective/Functional.
Numerical (Quantitative) Discrete Values obtained by counting; discrete integer support. Number of students in a class, days in a week.
Continuous Values obtained by physical measurement; can assume any real value within an interval. Height, weight, house prices, temperature.
