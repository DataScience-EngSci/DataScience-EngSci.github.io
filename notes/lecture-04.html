1. Foundational Statistical Lexicon: Population, Sample, Parameter, Statistic & Variable

Statistical inference bridges the gap between the entirety of a phenomenon and the limited subset we can realistically observe. The lecture establishes six precise foundational definitions:

Definition 1.1 (Key Statistical Terms)
  • Population: The entire collection of persons, things, or objects under scientific study.
  • Sample: A selected portion (subset) of the larger population chosen for empirical investigation.
  • Parameter: A fixed numerical characteristic or property of the whole population (e.g., the true population mean \(\mu\) or population variance \(\sigma^2\)). Parameters are typically unobservable.
  • Statistic: A numerical figure calculated directly from sample data that represents a property of that sample (e.g., the sample mean \(\bar{X}\)). A statistic is an estimator used to infer unknown population parameters.
    Slide Example: If we consider one mathematics class to be a sample of the population of all math classes at a university, then the average points earned by students in that single class is a statistic, whereas the average points earned across all math classes is the parameter.
  • Variable: A characteristic or measurement (usually denoted by capital letters such as \(X\) and \(Y\)) that can be determined for each member of a population. Variables are categorized into:
    • Numerical Variables: Take on numbers with equal, physical units (e.g., weight in pounds, time in hours, spending in dollars).
    • Categorical Variables: Assign members into distinct qualitative categories (e.g., political party affiliation, academic major, blood type).
  • Data: The actual recorded numerical values or categorical labels observed for the variable across the sampled units.

2. Concrete Case Study: School Supplies Spending at ABC College

The lecture presented a complete worked example to clarify the boundary between these key terms:

Lecture Case Study (ABC College Survey)

Scenario: An academic research committee wants to determine the average (mean) amount of money first-year college students spend at ABC College on school supplies (excluding textbooks). They conduct a survey of \(100\) randomly selected first-year students at the college. Three of those students report spending \(\$150\), \(\$200\), and \(\$225\), respectively.

Mapping to Statistical Terms:

  1. Population: All first-year college students enrolled at ABC College.
  2. Sample: The \(100\) first-year students randomly surveyed at ABC College.
  3. Parameter: The true average (mean) dollar amount spent on school supplies (excluding books) by all first-year students at ABC College (\(\mu\)).
  4. Statistic: The average dollar amount spent on school supplies by the \(100\) sampled first-year students (\(\bar{X}\)).
  5. Variable (\(X\)): The dollar amount spent on school supplies (excluding books) by an individual first-year student at ABC College. (This is a numerical continuous/discrete variable).
  6. Data: The specific recorded dollar amounts, such as \(\$150, \$200, \$225, \dots\).

3. Why Sample? Simple Random Sampling (SRS)

Gathering data from an entire population (conducting a complete census) is virtually impossible in most scientific settings due to exorbitant monetary costs, time constraints, and physical limitations. A representative sample allows us to perform accurate inference at a fraction of the cost.

Definition 3.1 (Simple Random Sampling - SRS) In a Simple Random Sample of size \(n\) from a population of size \(N\), every individual member has an equally likely chance of being chosen: \[ P(\text{Unit } i \text{ is selected}) = \frac{n}{N} \] Moreover, every possible subset of \(n\) distinct units has the exact same probability of forming the sample: \[ P(\mathcal{S}) = \frac{1}{\binom{N}{n}} \]

4. Stratified Random Sampling: Proportional Strata

When the population consists of heterogeneous sub-populations that are internally homogeneous, purely random sampling can accidentally over- or under-represent key groups. Stratified Sampling ensures balanced representation.

Definition 4.1 (Stratified Random Sampling) To select a stratified sample:
  1. Divide the total population into mutually exclusive, exhaustive subgroups called strata based on a known characteristic.
  2. Draw a proportionate simple random sample independently from each stratum.
Slide Example (College Department Stratification)

Suppose an administrator wants to survey student satisfaction across an entire university college. They first stratify (group) the student population by academic department (Engineering, Science, Humanities, Business). They then take a proportionate simple random sample from each department. If Engineering comprises \(30\%\) of the university student body, then \(30\%\) of the final sample is randomly selected from Engineering.

5. Cluster Sampling vs. Stratified Sampling

While stratified sampling samples from every stratum, geographical or administrative realities often make visiting every subgroup impractical.

Definition 5.1 (Cluster Sampling) To select a cluster sample:
  1. Divide the population into groups called clusters.
  2. Randomly select a subset of the clusters.
  3. Include all members from the selected clusters in the final sample.
Slide Example (Department Cluster Sampling)

In the same college setting, suppose researchers cannot travel across the entire campus. They randomly select four departments from the entire college population. Every single student enrolled in those four selected departments is surveyed.

Core Contrast: Stratified vs. Cluster Sampling
Feature Stratified Sampling Cluster Sampling
Selection Process Sample some members from all strata. Sample all members from some clusters.
Internal Structure Strata are homogeneous within, heterogeneous between. Clusters should be heterogeneous within (mini-populations).
Primary Advantage Maximizes estimation precision and eliminates between-strata variance. Minimizes operational data collection cost and logistics.

6. Systematic Sampling: Sampling Intervals & Phone Survey Example

When an ordered list of the population exists, Systematic Sampling provides a simple, highly effective alternative to drawing pure random numbers.

Definition 6.1 (Systematic Sampling) To choose a systematic sample of size \(n\) from a list of \(N\) members:
  1. Randomly choose a starting point \(r\) between \(1\) and the sampling step size \(k = \lfloor N/n \rfloor\).
  2. Select every \(k\)-th item on the list: \(r, \, r + k, \, r + 2k, \, \dots, \, r + (n-1)k\).
Slide Example (Telephone Directory Survey)

Suppose an investigator must conduct a telephone survey in a municipal district. The local phone directory contains \(N = 20,000\) residential listings, and the target sample size is \(n = 400\).

\[ k = \frac{N}{n} = \frac{20,000}{400} = 50 \]

The researcher picks a random starting number between \(1\) and \(50\) (say, \(17\)), and then selects every \(50\)-th name thereafter: the 17th, 67th, 117th, 167th, \dots, up to the 19,967th listing.

7. Sampling With/Without Replacement & Sampling Bias

Sampling With vs. Without Replacement

In sampling with replacement, once a member is selected, they are returned to the pool and can be chosen again. In sampling without replacement, each member can be chosen at most once.

Mathematical Note from Slides

Sampling without replacement instead of with replacement becomes a mathematically meaningful issue only when the population size \(N\) is small relative to \(n\). When \(N\) is very large compared to \(n\) (e.g. \(n/N < 0.05\)), the probability of re-selecting the same individual is negligible, and the draws behave essentially as independent, identically distributed (i.i.d.) observations.

Sampling Bias & Convenience Sampling

Definition 7.1 (Sampling Bias) A sampling bias is created when a sample is collected in such a way that some members of the intended population are systematically less likely to be chosen than others.
  • Convenience Sampling: A prominent source of sampling bias where data is collected from units that are easiest to reach (e.g. surveying friends, interviewing people walking past a campus cafe). Convenience samples are non-random and frequently yield heavily biased conclusions.
Summary Takeaway

Proper sampling design is the prerequisite for all sound statistical inference. If a sample is collected with systematic bias, no amount of complex machine learning or neural network architecture can recover the true population parameters. In Lecture 05, assuming an unbiased random sample \(X_1, \dots, X_n\), we formalize Point Estimation and Maximum Likelihood Estimation (MLE).
