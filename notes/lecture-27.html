1. Foundations & Motivations of Dimensionality Reduction

Slide 22 introduces Dimensionality Reduction, one of the cornerstones of unsupervised representation learning. In modern real-world datasets, feature dimensions \(n\) frequently range into thousands or millions (e.g., raw pixel arrays, genomic sequences, text vocabularies).

Core Motivations for Dimensionality Reduction:
  1. Data Compression: Storing high-dimensional data in compact representations, reducing memory and disk footprints (Slide 24 shows compressing two highly correlated features—e.g., pilot skill and pilot enjoyment—from \(\mathbb{R}^2\) down to a single latent coordinate \(z \in \mathbb{R}\); Slide 26 demonstrates compressing \(\mathbb{R}^3 \to \mathbb{R}^2\) and \(10{,}000 \to 1{,}000\)).
  2. Data Visualization: Human sensory perception is constrained to 2D and 3D. When analyzing a 50-variable dataset (Slides 27–29: Country economic indicators including GDP, per-capita GDP, HDI, life expectancy, poverty index, etc.), projecting from \(\mathbb{R}^{50} \to \mathbb{R}^2\) reveals macroscopic global structure, identifying country clusters and salient outliers (e.g., Singapore and the USA).
  3. Computational Acceleration: Reducing dimensions from \(300 \to 50\) or \(10{,}000 \to 1{,}000\) decreases the computational complexity of training subsequent machine learning models by orders of magnitude.

2. Principal Component Analysis (PCA): Dual Perspectives

Given an unlabelled dataset \(\{x^{(1)}, \dots, x^{(m)}\}\) where \(x^{(i)} \in \mathbb{R}^n\), Principal Component Analysis (PCA) seeks a lower \(k\)-dimensional linear subspace spanned by orthonormal basis vectors \(u_1, \dots, u_k \in \mathbb{R}^n\) (Slide 30–31).

Theorem 2.1 (The Fundamental Duality of PCA — Slide 36)
Fundamental Dual Equivalence: Maximizing the variance of projected coordinates is mathematically identical to minimizing the sum of squared orthogonal projection errors.
PCA can be defined through two distinct mathematical formulations that are provably equivalent:
  1. Maximum Variance Perspective: Find orthonormal directions \(u_1, \dots, u_k\) such that the variance of the projected data points is maximized.
  2. Minimum Reconstruction Error Perspective: Find a \(k\)-dimensional hyperplane such that the sum of squared orthogonal distances between the original points \(x^{(i)}\) and their projected points \(x_{\text{approx}}^{(i)}\) is minimized.
Fundamental Distinction: PCA is NOT Linear Regression (Slides 32–33):
Students often confuse PCA with Ordinary Least Squares (OLS) regression:
  • Linear Regression: Treats the target \(y\) as special. It minimizes the vertical Euclidean distance \((y^{(i)} - \theta^T x^{(i)})^2\) between true values and the regression line.
  • PCA: Treats all features \(x_1, \dots, x_n\) symmetrically with no designated response. It minimizes the orthogonal (perpendicular) Euclidean distance from each sample point to the projection subspace.

3. Mathematical Derivation: Lagrange Multipliers & Eigenvalue Problem

Slides 34–35 present the rigorous derivation of the first principal component \(u \in \mathbb{R}^n\) under the unit-norm constraint \(\|u\| = 1\).

Step-by-Step Derivation (Slides 34 & 35) Assume the dataset has been zero-centered such that sample mean \(\mu = \frac{1}{m}\sum x^{(i)} = 0\).
The scalar projection of a sample \(x^{(i)}\) onto a unit vector \(u\) (\(u^T u = 1\)) is \(x^{(i)T} u\). The variance of the projected data points is: \[ \begin{aligned} \text{Var}(u) &= \frac{1}{m} \sum_{i=1}^m \left( x^{(i)T} u \right)^2 \\ &= \frac{1}{m} \sum_{i=1}^m u^T x^{(i)} x^{(i)T} u \\ &= u^T \left( \frac{1}{m} \sum_{i=1}^m x^{(i)} x^{(i)T} \right) u \\ &= u^T \Sigma u \end{aligned} \] where \(\Sigma = \frac{1}{m} \sum_{i=1}^m x^{(i)} x^{(i)T} \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}\) is the empirical covariance matrix.

We maximize the projected variance subject to the normalization constraint: \[ \max_u \quad u^T \Sigma u \quad \text{subject to} \quad u^T u = 1 \]
Lagrange Derivation: Constrained variance maximization yields the eigensystem \(\Sigma u = \lambda u\), where \(u\) is the dominant eigenvector of \(\Sigma\).
Formulating the Lagrangian with multiplier \(\lambda\): \[ \mathcal{L}(u, \lambda) = u^T \Sigma u - \lambda(u^T u - 1) \] Taking the gradient with respect to \(u\) and equating to zero: \[ \nabla_u \mathcal{L} = 2 \Sigma u - 2 \lambda u = 0 \implies \Sigma u = \lambda u \] This reveals that \(u\) must be an eigenvector of the covariance matrix \(\Sigma\), with corresponding eigenvalue \(\lambda\)!

To determine which eigenvector maximizes variance, substitute \(\Sigma u = \lambda u\) back into the variance objective: \[ u^T \Sigma u = u^T (\lambda u) = \lambda (u^T u) = \lambda \] Therefore, the projected variance is exactly equal to the eigenvalue \(\lambda\). To maximize variance, we choose \(u_1\) as the eigenvector corresponding to the largest eigenvalue \(\lambda_1\)!
Generalization to \(k\) Dimensions (Slide 36)
Subspace Selection: To reduce dimensionality from \(n\) to \(k\), select \(u_1, u_2, \dots, u_k\) as the eigenvectors corresponding to the \(k\) largest eigenvalues of \(\Sigma\). These vectors define the principal orthogonal projection axes.
To reduce data to \(k\) dimensions while maintaining mutual orthogonality (\(u_i^T u_j = 0\) for \(i \ne j\)), we select the \(k\) eigenvectors \(u_1, \dots, u_k\) corresponding to the top \(k\) eigenvalues \(\lambda_1 \ge \lambda_2 \ge \dots \ge \lambda_k > 0\).

4. The Complete Computational Algorithm via SVD & Reconstruction

Slides 38–41 formalize the full computational algorithm using matrix notation and Singular Value Decomposition (SVD).

Algorithm 4.1 (Complete PCA Pipeline — Slide 40)
  1. Mean Normalization & Feature Scaling:
    Compute feature means: \(\mu_j = \frac{1}{m} \sum_{i=1}^m x_j^{(i)}\).
    Replace each feature: \(x_j^{(i)} := x_j^{(i)} - \mu_j\).
    If features are on different scales (e.g., GDP in dollars vs. life expectancy in years), scale by sample standard deviation: \(x_j^{(i)} := \frac{x_j^{(i)} - \mu_j}{s_j}\).
  2. Assemble Centered Data Matrix:
    Stack centered samples as rows: \[ X = \begin{bmatrix} (x^{(1)})^T \\ (x^{(2)})^T \\ \vdots \\ (x^{(m)})^T \end{bmatrix} \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n} \]
  3. Compute Empirical Covariance Matrix:
    \[ \Sigma = \frac{1}{m} X^T X \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n} \]
  4. Singular Value Decomposition (SVD):
    Compute `[U, S, V] = svd(Sigma);`. The columns of \(U \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}\) are the eigenvectors sorted in descending order of their corresponding singular values / eigenvalues: \[ U = \begin{bmatrix} | & | & & | \\ u^{(1)} & u^{(2)} & \dots & u^{(n)} \\ | & | & & | \end{bmatrix} \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n} \]
  5. Form Truncated Projection Matrix:
    Extract the first \(k\) columns: \[ U_{\text{reduce}} = \begin{bmatrix} | & & | \\ u^{(1)} & \dots & u^{(k)} \\ | & & | \end{bmatrix} \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times k} \]
  6. Project Data to Low-Dimensional Space:
    For a single point \(x \in \mathbb{R}^n\): \[ z = U_{\text{reduce}}^T x \in \mathbb{R}^k \] In vectorized form across the entire dataset: \[ Z = X U_{\text{reduce}} \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times k} \]
Reconstruction / Decompression (Slide 41) To reconstruct the high-dimensional point from its compressed representation \(z \in \mathbb{R}^k\): \[ x_{\text{approx}} = U_{\text{reduce}} z \in \mathbb{R}^n \] Notice the dimensions: \((n \times k) \times (k \times 1) = n \times 1\). The reconstructed vector \(x_{\text{approx}}\) is the exact orthogonal projection of the original point \(x\) onto the subspace spanned by the \(k\) principal components!

5. Choosing the Number of Components \(k\) via Singular Values

How do we choose the optimal target dimension \(k\)? Slides 42–44 formalize the Explained Variance Ratio criterion.

Definition 5.1 (Projection Error vs. Total Variation — Slide 42) Define:
  • Average Squared Projection Error (Distortion): \[ J = \frac{1}{m} \sum_{i=1}^m \|x^{(i)} - x_{\text{approx}}^{(i)}\|^2 \]
  • Total Variation in Data: \[ TV = \frac{1}{m} \sum_{i=1}^m \|x^{(i)}\|^2 \]
We seek the smallest integer \(k\) such that the relative projection error is below a chosen tolerance \(\epsilon\): \[ \frac{\frac{1}{m} \sum_{i=1}^m \|x^{(i)} - x_{\text{approx}}^{(i)}\|^2}{\frac{1}{m} \sum_{i=1}^m \|x^{(i)}\|^2} \le 0.01 \quad \iff \quad \text{99\% of variance is retained} \] Common industry thresholds:
  • \(\le 0.01 \implies 99\%\) variance retained.
  • \(\le 0.05 \implies 95\%\) variance retained.
  • \(\le 0.10 \implies 90\%\) variance retained.
Computational Shortcut via SVD Diagonal (Slides 43–44) Computing \(U_{\text{reduce}}, z^{(i)},\) and \(x_{\text{approx}}^{(i)}\) repeatedly for every candidate \(k \in \{1, 2, \dots, n\}\) would be horribly inefficient.

From the SVD `[U, S, V] = svd(Sigma)`, the matrix \(S \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}\) is diagonal with descending singular values \(S_{11} \ge S_{22} \ge \dots \ge S_{nn} \ge 0\). Remarkably: \[ \frac{\frac{1}{m} \sum_{i=1}^m \|x^{(i)} - x_{\text{approx}}^{(i)}\|^2}{\frac{1}{m} \sum_{i=1}^m \|x^{(i)}\|^2} = 1 - \frac{\sum_{i=1}^k S_{ii}}{\sum_{i=1}^n S_{ii}} \] Therefore, retaining \(99\%\) of variance is algebraically equivalent to checking: \[ \frac{\sum_{i=1}^k S_{ii}}{\sum_{i=1}^n S_{ii}} \ge 0.99 \] We compute SVD only once, sum the diagonal of \(S\), and instantly pick the smallest \(k\) satisfying the ratio!

6. Best Practices, Applications & Avoiding Critical Pitfalls

Critical Warning 1: Data Leakage in PCA Preprocessing (Slide 45) In supervised workflows with dataset \(\{(x^{(1)}, y^{(1)}), \dots, (x^{(m)}, y^{(m)})\}\):

Rule: The transformation mapping \(x \to z = U_{\text{reduce}}^T (x - \mu)\) must be computed solely on the TRAINING set!

Do NOT compute PCA on the combined dataset including test or cross-validation sets. Once \(\mu_{\text{train}}\) and \(U_{\text{reduce, train}}\) are obtained from training data alone, apply the exact same transformation to cross-validation samples \(x_{\text{cv}}\) and test samples \(x_{\text{test}}\). Violating this creates subtle, catastrophic data leakage.
Critical Warning 2: Bad Use of PCA: Preventing Overfitting (Slide 47):
A frequent fallacy is using PCA to reduce features to avoid overfitting. The reasoning seems plausible: fewer features means a simpler hypothesis class, which reduces variance.
Why this is flawed: PCA reduces dimensionality completely blindly without ever inspecting the target labels \(y\)! It discards directions of low variance. However, the subtle signal that distinguishes classes might align precisely with one of those discarded low-variance directions!

Correct Practice: To address overfitting, keep all features and use regularization (\(L_2\) Ridge penalty \(\frac{\lambda}{2m} \sum \theta_j^2\) or \(L_1\) Lasso). Regularization uses the labels to downweight irrelevant features gracefully.

Rule of Thumb (Slide 48): Before attempting PCA, always train your baseline model on the original, raw data \(x^{(i)}\). Only adopt PCA if raw training is computationally intractable or memory-bound.
Applications & Kernel PCA (Slide 46)
  • Eigenfaces in Computer Vision: Compressing facial photographs from \(100 \times 100 = 10{,}000\) pixels down to \(k = 1{,}000\) principal components (reducing dimensionality by an order of magnitude while preserving discriminative fidelity).
  • Noise Filtering: Discarding trailing principal components with negligible eigenvalues effectively strips uncorrelated sensor noise.
  • Kernel PCA: By applying a non-linear feature map \(x \to \phi(x)\), PCA can discover non-linear manifolds through kernel evaluations \(K(x^{(i)}, x^{(j)})\).

7. Supervised Dimensionality Reduction: Fisher's Linear Discriminant (Two-Class)

Slides 50–62 introduce Linear Discriminant Analysis (LDA), also known as Fisher's Linear Discriminant.

Supervised vs. Unsupervised Dimensionality Reduction (Slide 51)
  • PCA is Unsupervised: It ignores class labels and seeks directions that are efficient for representation (maximizing total variance).
  • LDA is Supervised: It explicitly uses class labels to seek directions that are efficient for discrimination (maximizing class separability).
Slide 51 & Slide 54 illustrate that projecting onto the direction of maximum variance (PCA) can completely overlap two distinct classes, whereas LDA finds the direction separating them cleanly!
Fisher's Criterion Formulation for Two Classes (Slides 55–60) Let dataset \(\mathcal{D}\) be partitioned into two classes \(\mathcal{C}_1\) and \(\mathcal{C}_2\) of sizes \(N_1\) and \(N_2\). We project each point onto a unit vector \(w \in \mathbb{R}^n\): \[ y = w^T x \]
  1. Class Means in Original Space: \(\mu_1 = \frac{1}{N_1}\sum_{x \in \mathcal{C}_1} x\), \quad \(\mu_2 = \frac{1}{N_2}\sum_{x \in \mathcal{C}_2} x\).
    Projected Class Means: \(\tilde{\mu}_1 = w^T \mu_1\), \quad \(\tilde{\mu}_2 = w^T \mu_2\).
  2. Between-Class Separation: The distance between projected means is: \[ (\tilde{\mu}_1 - \tilde{\mu}_2)^2 = (w^T \mu_1 - w^T \mu_2)^2 = w^T (\mu_1 - \mu_2)(\mu_1 - \mu_2)^T w = w^T S_B w \] where \(S_B = (\mu_1 - \mu_2)(\mu_1 - \mu_2)^T \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}\) is the Between-Class Scatter Matrix (Slide 59).
  3. Within-Class Scatter: To avoid class overlap, the variance of projected points within each class must be minimized (Slide 57–58): \[ \tilde{s}_1^2 = \sum_{y \in \mathcal{C}_1} (y - \tilde{\mu}_1)^2 = w^T S_1 w, \quad \tilde{s}_2^2 = \sum_{y \in \mathcal{C}_2} (y - \tilde{\mu}_2)^2 = w^T S_2 w \] where \(S_i = \sum_{x \in \mathcal{C}_i} (x - \mu_i)(x - \mu_i)^T\). The total within-class scatter is: \[ \tilde{s}_1^2 + \tilde{s}_2^2 = w^T (S_1 + S_2) w = w^T S_W w \] where \(S_W = S_1 + S_2 \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}\) is the Within-Class Scatter Matrix.
Fisher's Objective: Maximize the ratio of between-class separation to within-class spread (Slide 60): \[ \max_w \quad J(w) = \frac{(\tilde{\mu}_1 - \tilde{\mu}_2)^2}{\tilde{s}_1^2 + \tilde{s}_2^2} = \frac{w^T S_B w}{w^T S_W w} \] Differentiating \(J(w)\) with respect to \(w\) and setting to zero yields the generalized eigenvalue problem: \[ S_B w = J(w) S_W w \implies S_W^{-1} S_B w = \lambda w \] Since \(S_B w = (\mu_1 - \mu_2)(\mu_1 - \mu_2)^T w\) is always aligned in the direction of \((\mu_1 - \mu_2)\), the scalar factor \((\mu_1 - \mu_2)^T w\) merely scales \(w\). Therefore, the optimal projection vector is: \[ w^* = S_W^{-1} (\mu_1 - \mu_2) \]
Optimal Projection Direction: The optimal projection vector \(w^*\) is the eigenvector corresponding to the maximum eigenvalue of \(S_W^{-1} S_B\).
Numerical Example from Slide 61 Consider two 2D classes (\(n = 2\)) with 5 samples each: \[ X_1 = \begin{bmatrix} 4 & 2 \\ 2 & 4 \\ 2 & 3 \\ 3 & 6 \\ 4 & 4 \end{bmatrix}, \quad X_2 = \begin{bmatrix} 9 & 10 \\ 6 & 8 \\ 9 & 5 \\ 8 & 7 \\ 10 & 8 \end{bmatrix} \] We compute the class centroids \(\mu_1 = \begin{bmatrix} 3.0 \\ 3.8 \end{bmatrix}\) and \(\mu_2 = \begin{bmatrix} 8.4 \\ 7.6 \end{bmatrix}\).
Computing scatter matrices \(S_1, S_2 \implies S_W = S_1 + S_2\), inverting \(S_W\), and multiplying by \((\mu_1 - \mu_2)\) gives the optimal 1D discriminant projection line \(w^* = S_W^{-1}(\mu_1 - \mu_2)\).

8. Multi-Class LDA (\(C > 2\)) & The Determinant Ratio Criterion

Slides 63–67 generalize Fisher's method to \(C > 2\) classes. In this setting, the projection is performed by a matrix \(W \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times (C-1)}\), reducing the dimension from \(n\) to at most \(C - 1\).

Multi-Class Scatter Matrices (Slide 65) Let \(\mu = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^N x = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{c=1}^C N_c \mu_c\) be the global sample mean across all \(N\) observations.
  • Within-Class Scatter Matrix: \[ S_W = \sum_{c=1}^C S_c = \sum_{c=1}^C \sum_{x \in \mathcal{C}_c} (x - \mu_c)(x - \mu_c)^T \]
  • Between-Class Scatter Matrix: \[ S_B = \sum_{c=1}^C N_c (\mu_c - \mu)(\mu_c - \mu)^T \]
The Determinant Ratio Criterion (Slide 67) For projection matrix \(W = [w_1 \mid w_2 \mid \dots \mid w_{C-1}] \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times (C-1)}\), the projected scatter matrices are: \[ \tilde{S}_W = W^T S_W W, \quad \tilde{S}_B = W^T S_B W \] Because scalar division is undefined for matrices, the multi-class Fisher criterion maximizes the ratio of determinants (measuring hyper-ellipsoidal scatter volumes): \[ J(W) = \frac{|\tilde{S}_B|}{|\tilde{S}_W|} = \frac{|W^T S_B W|}{|W^T S_W W|} \] Setting \(\frac{dJ}{dW} = 0\) yields the generalized eigenvalue equation: \[ S_W^{-1} S_B w_i = \lambda_i w_i \quad (i = 1, \dots, C-1) \]
Multi-Class Projection Columns: The columns of \(W^*\) are the \(C-1\) eigenvectors corresponding to the \(C-1\) largest eigenvalues of \(S_W^{-1} S_B\).
The columns of the optimal projection matrix \(W^*\) are the \(C - 1\) eigenvectors corresponding to the \(C - 1\) largest eigenvalues of \(S_W^{-1} S_B\)!

9. Assumptions, Limitations & When LDA Fails

Slide 69 details the core theoretical assumptions and failure modes of Linear Discriminant Analysis.

Core Theoretical Assumptions & Pathologies (Slide 69)
Gaussian Class-Conditional Assumption: Linear Discriminant Analysis formally assumes each class is normally distributed with a shared covariance matrix.
  1. Gaussian Assumption: LDA assumes each class is normally distributed: \(p(x \mid y = c) \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu_c, \Sigma)\).
  2. Equal Covariance Matrices (Homoscedasticity): Crucially, LDA assumes all classes share the identical covariance matrix \(\Sigma_1 = \dots = \Sigma_C = \Sigma\).
  3. Maximum Subspace Rank: Because \(S_B\) is the sum of \(C\) rank-1 outer products with constraint \(\sum N_c(\mu_c - \mu) = 0\), the rank of \(S_B\) is at most \(C - 1\). Therefore, the maximum number of dimensions LDA can reduce data to is \(C - 1\)! For binary classification (\(C = 2\)), LDA can only project to a 1D line!
Failure Mode (Variance Discrepancies): When class differences stem from variance structure rather than mean separation (i.e. \(\mu_1 \approx \mu_2\)), between-class scatter vanishes (\(S_B \approx 0\)), causing LDA to fail completely.
When LDA Fails: If two classes share the same mean (\(\mu_1 = \mu_2\)) but differ significantly in variance (e.g., a tight inner sphere surrounded by an outer ring), the between-class scatter vanishes: \[ S_B = (\mu_1 - \mu_2)(\mu_1 - \mu_2)^T = 0 \] LDA collapses completely and is utterly unable to separate the classes!

10. Grand Synthesis: PCA + LDA Pipeline, GDA, and QDA

Slide 70 concludes Lecture 27 with an overarching synthesis connecting LDA, PCA, GDA, and QDA into an unified engineering taxonomy.

The PCA + LDA Two-Stage Pipeline (Slide 70)
Small Sample Size Solution (Fisherfaces): When feature dimension exceeds sample count (\(n > N\)), \(S_W\) is singular. The canonical solution is a PCA-LDA pipeline: first apply PCA to reduce dimension to \(N - C\), then perform LDA.
In high-dimensional regimes where the number of features exceeds the sample size (\(n > m\), such as computer vision where each image has \(10{,}000\) pixels but only 500 images exist), the within-class scatter matrix \(S_W\) is rank-deficient and singular. Its inverse \(S_W^{-1}\) does not exist!

The Solution (The Fisherfaces Paradigm):
  1. Apply unsupervised PCA to project the raw \(n\)-dimensional data down to an intermediate dimension \(m - C\) where the covariance matrix is guaranteed to be full-rank.
  2. Apply supervised LDA on the PCA-projected features to compress further to \(C - 1\) highly discriminative coordinates!
Taxonomy of Generative Classifiers: LDA vs. QDA (Slide 70)
Model Covariance Assumption Decision Boundary Parameter Count
Linear Discriminant Analysis (LDA) \(\Sigma_1 = \Sigma_2 = \dots = \Sigma_k\) (Equal) Linear (Hyperplane) \(O(n^2 + k \cdot n)\)
Quadratic Discriminant Analysis (QDA) \(\Sigma_k\) separate for each class (Unequal) Quadratic (Conic Surface) \(O(k \cdot n^2)\)

Pipeline Summary (Slide 70): For high-dimensional data, the optimal workflow is: \[ \text{High Dimensional Inputs } x \in \mathbb{R}^n \xrightarrow{\text{PCA}} z \in \mathbb{R}^{n'} \xrightarrow{\text{LDA}} \tilde{z} \in \mathbb{R}^{C-1} \xrightarrow{\text{GDA}} \hat{y} \]
