1. Mathematical Expectation & The LOTUS Theorem

The expectation (or mathematical mean) represents the center of mass of a probability distribution.

Definition 1.1 (Expectation)
  • Discrete Random Variable: \[ E[X] \triangleq \sum_{x \in \text{Val}(X)} x \, p_X(x) \]
  • Continuous Random Variable: \[ E[X] \triangleq \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} x \, f_X(x) \, dx \]
Theorem 1.2 (Law of the Unconscious Statistician - LOTUS) To compute the expected value of a transformed random variable \(g(X)\), we do not need to derive the distribution of \(g(X)\); we integrate or sum directly against the distribution of \(X\):
  • Discrete: \[ E[g(X)] = \sum_{x \in \text{Val}(X)} g(x) \, p_X(x) \]
  • Continuous: \[ E[g(X)] = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} g(x) \, f_X(x) \, dx \]
Proposition 1.3 (Linearity of Expectation) Expectation is a strictly linear operator. For any constants \(a, \alpha, \beta \in \mathbb{R}\) and functions \(g_1, g_2\):
  1. \(E[a] = a\), since \(\int_{-\infty}^\infty a f_X(x) dx = a \int_{-\infty}^\infty f_X(x) dx = a\).
  2. \(E[a f(X)] = a E[f(X)]\).
  3. \(E[\alpha g_1(X) + \beta g_2(X)] = \alpha E[g_1(X)] + \beta E[g_2(X)]\).
  4. Indicator Expectation: For the indicator random variable \(\mathbf{1}\{X = k\}\): \[ E[\mathbf{1}\{X = k\}] = 1 \cdot P(X = k) + 0 \cdot P(X \neq k) = P(X = k) \]

2. Variance & Derivation for \(\text{Uniform}(0, 1)\)

While expectation characterizes central tendency, variance quantifies the expected squared dispersion around the mean:

Definition 2.1 (Variance & Scaling Properties) \[ \text{Var}(X) \triangleq E\big[(X - E[X])^2\big] = E[X^2] - (E[X])^2 \] Under scalar multiplication and constant shifts: \[ \text{Var}(a X + b) = a^2 \, \text{Var}(X), \qquad \text{Var}(a f(X)) = a^2 \, \text{Var}(f(X)) \]
Explicit Derivation from Slides (\(X \sim \text{Uniform}(0, 1)\))

Let \(X \sim \text{Uniform}(0, 1)\). Its density is \(f_X(x) = 1\) for \(x \in [0, 1]\) and \(0\) otherwise.

  1. Mean: \[ E[X] = \int_0^1 x \cdot 1 \, dx = \left[ \frac{x^2}{2} \right]_0^1 = \frac{1}{2} \]
  2. Second Moment: \[ E[X^2] = \int_0^1 x^2 \cdot 1 \, dx = \left[ \frac{x^3}{3} \right]_0^1 = \frac{1}{3} \]
  3. Variance: \[ \text{Var}(X) = E[X^2] - (E[X])^2 = \frac{1}{3} - \left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^2 = \frac{1}{3} - \frac{1}{4} = \frac{1}{12} \]

3. Canonical Discrete & Continuous Distributions

The lecture systematically cataloged the workhorse parametric probability families frequently used in data science:

Discrete Families

Discrete Distributions
  1. Bernoulli Distribution: A single binary trial with success probability \(p \in [0, 1]\): \[ P_X(x) = p^x (1-p)^{1-x} = \begin{cases} p & x = 1 \text{ (Heads)} \\ 1-p & x = 0 \text{ (Tails)} \\ 0 & \text{otherwise} \end{cases} \] \[ E[X] = p, \qquad \text{Var}(X) = E[X^2] - E[X]^2 = p - p^2 = p(1-p) \]
  2. Binomial Distribution: Number of successes in \(n\) independent Bernoulli\((p)\) trials: \[ X \sim \text{Binomial}(n, p), \qquad P_X(x) = \binom{n}{x} p^x (1-p)^{n-x}, \quad x \in \{0, 1, \dots, n\} \] \[ E[X] = np, \qquad \text{Var}(X) = np(1-p) \]
  3. Geometric Distribution: Number of independent trials until the first success occurs: \[ X \sim \text{Geometric}(p), \qquad p(x) = p(1-p)^{x-1}, \quad x \in \{1, 2, 3, \dots\} \] \[ E[X] = \frac{1}{p}, \qquad \text{Var}(X) = \frac{1-p}{p^2} \]
  4. Poisson Distribution: Counts the occurrences of events that occur independently at a constant average rate \(\lambda > 0\) (often used for modeling rare events): \[ X \sim \text{Poisson}(\lambda), \qquad p(x) = \frac{e^{-\lambda} \lambda^x}{x!}, \quad x \in \{0, 1, 2, \dots\} \] \[ E[X] = \lambda, \qquad \text{Var}(X) = \lambda \]

Continuous Families

Continuous Distributions
  1. Uniform Distribution: Equal probability density across interval \([a, b]\): \[ X \sim \text{Uniform}(a, b), \qquad f_X(x) = \begin{cases} \frac{1}{b-a} & a \le x \le b \\ 0 & \text{otherwise} \end{cases} \] \[ E[X] = \frac{a+b}{2}, \qquad \text{Var}(X) = \frac{(b-a)^2}{12} \]
  2. Exponential Distribution: Time elapsed until the occurrence of an event in a Poisson process: \[ X \sim \text{Exponential}(\lambda), \qquad f(x) = \begin{cases} \lambda e^{-\lambda x} & x \ge 0 \\ 0 & \text{otherwise} \end{cases} \quad (\lambda > 0) \] \[ E[X] = \frac{1}{\lambda}, \qquad \text{Var}(X) = \frac{1}{\lambda^2} \]
  3. Normal (Gaussian) Distribution: The bell-shaped distribution that underpins classical statistics: \[ X \sim \text{Normal}(\mu, \sigma^2), \qquad f_X(x) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^2}} \exp\left(-\frac{(x-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}\right) \] Symmetric around the mean \(\mu\), with dispersion governed by standard deviation \(\sigma\).

4. Bivariate Random Variables: Joint CDF, PMF & PDF

In applications, we frequently measure multiple characteristics simultaneously.

Slide Motivating Example (Two Dependent Random Variables)

Suppose we flip a coin \(10\) times. We may be simultaneously interested in two distinct variables:

  • Variable \(A\): The total count of heads obtained in the \(10\) flips.
  • Variable \(B\): The maximum length of consecutive heads (the longest streak).

Examining \(A\) and \(B\) separately with univariate CDFs \(F_A(a)\) and \(F_B(b)\) discards the correlation and dependency between them. To capture their joint probabilistic structure, we define the Joint CDF.

Definition 4.1 (Joint CDF & Properties) For any two random variables \(X\) and \(Y\), the Joint Cumulative Distribution Function is: \[ F_{XY}(x, y) \triangleq P(X \le x, Y \le y) \] satisfying the fundamental boundary conditions:
  1. \(0 \le F_{XY}(x, y) \le 1\).
  2. \(\lim_{x \to \infty, y \to \infty} F_{XY}(x, y) = 1\).
  3. \(\lim_{x \to -\infty} F_{XY}(x, y) = \lim_{y \to -\infty} F_{XY}(x, y) = 0\).
Definition 4.2 (Joint PMF & Joint PDF)
  • Discrete Joint PMF: \[ P_{XY}(x, y) \triangleq P(X = x, Y = y), \qquad \sum_{x \in \text{Val}(X)} \sum_{y \in \text{Val}(Y)} P_{XY}(x, y) = 1 \]
  • Continuous Joint PDF: The mixed second partial derivative of the Joint CDF: \[ f_{XY}(x, y) \triangleq \frac{\partial^2 F_{XY}(x, y)}{\partial x \partial y} \] where: \[ \iint_{\mathbb{R}^2} f_{XY}(x, y) \, dx \, dy = 1, \qquad P\big((X, Y) \in A\big) = \iint_A f_{XY}(x, y) \, dx \, dy \] Warning: Just as in the univariate case, \(f_{XY}(x, y) \neq P(X=x, Y=y)\).

5. Marginal Distributions & Conditional Distributions

Given a joint distribution, we can recover the individual behavior of one variable by "summing out" or "integrating out" the other variable. This operation is called marginalization.

Definition 5.1 (Marginal Distributions)
  • Marginal CDF: \[ F_X(x) = \lim_{y \to \infty} F_{XY}(x, y), \qquad F_Y(y) = \lim_{x \to \infty} F_{XY}(x, y) \]
  • Marginal PMF (Discrete): \[ P_X(x) = \sum_{y \in \text{Val}(Y)} P_{XY}(x, y), \qquad P_Y(y) = \sum_{x \in \text{Val}(X)} P_{XY}(x, y) \]
  • Marginal PDF (Continuous): \[ f_X(x) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} f_{XY}(x, y) \, dy, \qquad f_Y(y) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} f_{XY}(x, y) \, dx \]
Definition 5.2 (Conditional Distributions) Conditioning describes the distribution of \(Y\) once we observe that \(X = x\):
  • Discrete Conditional PMF: For \(P_X(x) > 0\): \[ P_{Y \mid X}(y \mid x) \triangleq \frac{P_{XY}(x, y)}{P_X(x)} \]
  • Continuous Conditional PDF: For \(f_X(x) > 0\): \[ f_{Y \mid X}(y \mid x) \triangleq \frac{f_{XY}(x, y)}{f_X(x)} \]

6. Bayes' Rule for Random Variables

Combining conditional definitions with marginalization yields Bayes' Rule for Random Variables:

Theorem 6.1 (Bayes' Rule for Random Variables)
  • Discrete Formulation: \[ P_{Y \mid X}(y \mid x) = \frac{P_{XY}(x, y)}{P_X(x)} = \frac{P_{X \mid Y}(x \mid y) \, P_Y(y)}{\sum_{y' \in \text{Val}(Y)} P_{X \mid Y}(x \mid y') \, P_Y(y')} \] where \(P_Y(y)\) is the prior distribution and \(P_{Y \mid X}(y \mid x)\) is the posterior distribution given evidence \(x\).
  • Continuous Formulation: \[ f_{Y \mid X}(y \mid x) = \frac{f_{XY}(x, y)}{f_X(x)} = \frac{f_{X \mid Y}(x \mid y) \, f_Y(y)}{\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} f_{X \mid Y}(x \mid y') \, f_Y(y') \, dy'} \]

7. Independence vs. Uncorrelatedness (The Crucial Counterexample)

Definition 7.1 (Statistical Independence of Random Variables) Two random variables \(X\) and \(Y\) are statistically independent if and only if their joint distribution factors into the product of their marginals: \[ F_{XY}(x, y) = F_X(x) \, F_Y(y) \quad \forall x, y \in \mathbb{R} \] Equivalently:
  • Discrete: \(P_{XY}(x, y) = P_X(x) \, P_Y(y) \iff P_{Y \mid X}(y \mid x) = P_Y(y)\).
  • Continuous: \(f_{XY}(x, y) = f_X(x) \, f_Y(y) \iff f_{Y \mid X}(y \mid x) = f_Y(y)\).
Physical Interpretation: Knowing the realized value of \(X\) has absolutely zero effect on the distribution of \(Y\).
Proposition 7.2 (Properties of Independent Variables) If \(X\) and \(Y\) are independent:
  1. For any sets \(A, B \subseteq \mathbb{R}\): \[ P(X \in A, Y \in B) = P(X \in A) \, P(Y \in B) \]
  2. Any transformations \(g(X)\) and \(h(Y)\) are also independent, and expectations factor: \[ E\big[g(X) \, h(Y)\big] = E\big[g(X)\big] \, E\big[h(Y)\big] \]

Covariance and Variance of Sums

Definition 7.3 (Covariance) \[ \text{Cov}(X, Y) \triangleq E\big[(X - E[X])(Y - E[Y])\big] = E[XY] - E[X] \, E[Y] \] Variables with \(\text{Cov}(X, Y) = 0\) are called uncorrelated. The variance of a sum is given by: \[ \text{Var}(X + Y) = \text{Var}(X) + \text{Var}(Y) + 2\,\text{Cov}(X, Y) \] When \(X\) and \(Y\) are independent, \(\text{Cov}(X, Y) = 0\), so \(\text{Var}(X + Y) = \text{Var}(X) + \text{Var}(Y)\).
Crucial Counterexample: Uncorrelatedness \(\centernot\implies\) Independence

While independence strictly implies zero covariance, the converse is fundamentally false. The professor presented the following exact counterexample (marked with a prominent red cross on slide 27):

Let \(X \sim \text{Uniform}(-1, 1)\), and define \(Y = X^2\).

  • Are they independent? Absolutely not. Knowing \(X\) determines \(Y\) completely (\(Y\) is a deterministic function of \(X\)).
  • What is their covariance?
    1. By symmetry of \(\text{Uniform}(-1, 1)\): \(E[X] = \int_{-1}^1 x \cdot \frac{1}{2} \, dx = 0\).
    2. Compute the joint product expectation: \[ E[XY] = E[X \cdot X^2] = E[X^3] = \int_{-1}^1 x^3 \cdot \frac{1}{2} \, dx = 0 \] (since \(x^3\) is an odd function integrated over the symmetric interval \([-1, 1]\)).
    3. Evaluate covariance: \[ \text{Cov}(X, Y) = E[XY] - E[X] \, E[Y] = 0 - (0 \cdot E[Y]) = 0 \]

Conclusion: \(X\) and \(Y\) have zero covariance (\(\text{Cov}(X, Y) = 0\), completely uncorrelated), yet they are completely dependent! Covariance measures only linear association, missing nonlinear relationships entirely.

8. Multiple Random Variables (\(n\)-Dimensional Random Vectors)

The slide deck concludes by extending all definitions from bivariate pairs to general \(n\)-dimensional random vectors \(\mathbf{X} = (X_1, X_2, \dots, X_n)^T\):

Definition 8.1 (\(n\)-Dimensional Distributions)
  • Joint CDF: \[ F_{X_1, X_2, \dots, X_n}(x_1, x_2, \dots, x_n) \triangleq P(X_1 \le x_1, X_2 \le x_2, \dots, X_n \le x_n) \]
  • Joint Continuous PDF: The \(n\)-th mixed partial derivative: \[ f_{X_1, X_2, \dots, X_n}(x_1, x_2, \dots, x_n) = \frac{\partial^n F_{X_1, \dots, X_n}(x_1, \dots, x_n)}{\partial x_1 \, \partial x_2 \dots \partial x_n} \]
  • Marginal Density: Integrating out the remaining \(n-1\) variables: \[ f_{X_1}(x_1) = \int \dots \int_{\mathbb{R}^{n-1}} f_{X_1, X_2, \dots, X_n}(x_1, x_2, \dots, x_n) \, dx_2 \, dx_3 \dots dx_n \]
  • Conditional Density: Conditioning one variable on all remaining \(n-1\) variables: \[ f_{X_1 \mid X_2, \dots, X_n}(x_1 \mid x_2, \dots, x_n) = \frac{f_{X_1, X_2, \dots, X_n}(x_1, x_2, \dots, x_n)}{f_{X_2, \dots, X_n}(x_2, \dots, x_n)} \]
