1. Mean Squared Error (MSE) & Bias-Variance Decomposition

When evaluating competing candidate estimators \(\hat{\theta}_1\) and \(\hat{\theta}_2\), unbiasedness alone is insufficient: an unbiased estimator might have massive variance, making individual estimates wild. We require a criterion balancing accuracy (low bias) with precision (low variance).

Definition 1.1 (Mean Squared Error - MSE) The Mean Squared Error of an estimator \(\hat{\theta}\) for parameter \(\theta\) is defined as: \[ \text{MSE}(\hat{\theta}) = E\left[(\hat{\theta} - \theta)^2\right] \]
Theorem 1.2 (Bias-Variance Decomposition of MSE) For any estimator \(\hat{\theta}\): \[ \text{MSE}(\hat{\theta}) = \text{Var}(\hat{\theta}) + \left[\text{Bias}(\hat{\theta})\right]^2 \]
Comparing Three Estimators (Slide 7.1 Intuition)

Consider three competing estimators of an unknown parameter \(\theta\) represented by sampling distributions (curves):

  • Black Curve: Unbiased (\(E[\hat{\theta}] = \theta\)), but with large variance (wide spread).
  • Blue Curve: Unbiased (\(E[\hat{\theta}] = \theta\)), with moderate variance (\(\text{Var} = 16\)). Between the black and blue curves, any data scientist should prefer the blue curve.
  • Red Curve: Biased (shifted away from \(\theta\) by \(\text{Bias} = 2\)), but with very small variance (\(\text{Var} = 4\)).

How do we compare the blue and red curves? The smaller variance of the red estimator is not alone a measure of how close it is to \(\theta\), because it is systematically centered in the wrong location!

Numerical MSE Comparison from Slide 7.1:

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{\textbf{Blue Estimator (Unbiased):}} & \quad \text{MSE}(\hat{\theta}_{\text{blue}}) = \text{Var} + \text{Bias}^2 = 16 + 0^2 = 16 \\ \text{\textbf{Red Estimator (Biased):}} & \quad \text{MSE}(\hat{\theta}_{\text{red}}) = \text{Var} + \text{Bias}^2 = 4 + (2)^2 = 4 + 4 = 8 \end{aligned} \]

Because \(\text{MSE}_{\text{red}} = 8 < 16 = \text{MSE}_{\text{blue}}\), the biased red estimator is on average closer to the true parameter \(\theta\) than the unbiased blue estimator! Rule: If you have multiple candidate estimators and at least one is biased, compare them using Mean Squared Error.

2. Relative Efficiency of Estimators

Definition 2.1 (Relative Efficiency) Let \(\hat{\theta}_1\) and \(\hat{\theta}_2\) be two unbiased estimators of \(\theta\). The relative efficiency of \(\hat{\theta}_1\) with respect to \(\hat{\theta}_2\) is: \[ \text{Eff}(\hat{\theta}_1, \hat{\theta}_2) = \frac{\text{Var}(\hat{\theta}_2)}{\text{Var}(\hat{\theta}_1)} \] If \(\text{Eff}(\hat{\theta}_1, \hat{\theta}_2) > 1\), \(\hat{\theta}_1\) has strictly smaller variance and is considered statistically more efficient.

3. The Score Function & Fisher Information

How much information does an empirical sample \(\mathbf{X} = (X_1, \dots, X_n)\) carry about an unknown parameter \(\theta\)? This is formalized by Fisher Information.

Definition 3.1 (Score Function) The score function \(S(\mathbf{X}; \theta)\) is the gradient of the log-likelihood: \[ S(\mathbf{X}; \theta) = \frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}\ln f(\mathbf{X}; \theta) \]
Lemma 3.2 (The Expected Score is Zero) Under regularity conditions permitting differentiation under the integral: \[ E[S(\mathbf{X}; \theta)] = E\left[\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}\ln f(\mathbf{X}; \theta)\right] = 0 \]
Proof Recall that the total probability density integrates to 1: \[ \int f(\mathbf{x}; \theta) \, d\mathbf{x} = 1 \] Differentiating both sides with respect to \(\theta\): \[ \frac{\partial}{\partial\theta} \int f(\mathbf{x}; \theta) \, d\mathbf{x} = \int \frac{\partial}{\partial\theta} f(\mathbf{x}; \theta) \, d\mathbf{x} = 0 \] Using the identity \(\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}f = f \cdot \frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}\ln f\): \[ \int \left(\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}\ln f(\mathbf{x}; \theta)\right) f(\mathbf{x}; \theta) \, d\mathbf{x} = E\left[\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}\ln f(\mathbf{X}; \theta)\right] = 0 \quad \blacksquare \]
Definition 3.3 (Fisher Information) The Fisher Information \(I_n(\theta)\) contained in sample \(\mathbf{X}\) is the variance of the score function: \[ I_n(\theta) = \text{Var}\left(S(\mathbf{X}; \theta)\right) = E\left[\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}\ln f(\mathbf{X}; \theta)\right)^2\right] \]

4. The Cramér-Rao Lower Bound (CRLB) Theorem

The Cramér-Rao Lower Bound represents an inescapable physical boundary in statistical estimation: it provides a strict lower bound on the variance achievable by any unbiased estimator.

Theorem 4.1 (Cramér-Rao Lower Bound) Let \(X_1, \dots, X_n \sim f(x; \theta)\) satisfy the regularity conditions (the support of \(f\) does not depend on \(\theta\), and differentiation under the integral is valid). Let \(T(\mathbf{X})\) be any unbiased estimator of a function \(\tau(\theta)\) (i.e., \(E[T] = \tau(\theta)\)). Then: \[ \text{Var}(T(\mathbf{X})) \ge \frac{[\tau'(\theta)]^2}{I_n(\theta)} \] In the special case of estimating \(\theta\) directly (\(\tau(\theta) = \theta \implies \tau'(\theta) = 1\)): \[ \text{Var}(\hat{\theta}) \ge \frac{1}{I_n(\theta)} \]

5. Rigorous Proof via Cauchy-Schwarz Inequality

Proof of Theorem 4.1 Consider the two random variables: \[ U = T(\mathbf{X}) \qquad \text{and} \qquad V = S(\mathbf{X}; \theta) = \frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}\ln f(\mathbf{X}; \theta) \] From Lemma 3.2, \(E[V] = 0\), so: \[ \text{Cov}(U, V) = E[UV] - E[U]\underbrace{E[V]}_{0} = E\left[T(\mathbf{X}) \frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}\ln f(\mathbf{X}; \theta)\right] \] Expanding this expectation as an integral: \[ \text{Cov}(U, V) = \int T(\mathbf{x}) \left(\frac{1}{f(\mathbf{x}; \theta)} \frac{\partial f(\mathbf{x}; \theta)}{\partial\theta}\right) f(\mathbf{x}; \theta) \, d\mathbf{x} = \int T(\mathbf{x}) \frac{\partial}{\partial\theta} f(\mathbf{x}; \theta) \, d\mathbf{x} \] Under the regularity condition, we interchange differentiation and integration: \[ \text{Cov}(U, V) = \frac{\partial}{\partial\theta} \int T(\mathbf{x}) f(\mathbf{x}; \theta) \, d\mathbf{x} = \frac{\partial}{\partial\theta} E[T(\mathbf{X})] \] Since \(T\) is an unbiased estimator of \(\tau(\theta)\), \(E[T(\mathbf{X})] = \tau(\theta)\). Therefore: \[ \text{Cov}(U, V) = \frac{\partial}{\partial\theta} \tau(\theta) = \tau'(\theta) \] Now, recall the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality for covariance: \[ [\text{Cov}(U, V)]^2 \le \text{Var}(U) \cdot \text{Var}(V) \] Substituting our derived terms: \[ [\tau'(\theta)]^2 \le \text{Var}(T(\mathbf{X})) \cdot I_n(\theta) \] Dividing by the positive quantity \(I_n(\theta) > 0\): \[ \text{Var}(T(\mathbf{X})) \ge \frac{[\tau'(\theta)]^2}{I_n(\theta)} \quad \blacksquare \]

6. Uniformly Minimum Variance Unbiased Estimators (UMVUE) & Regularity Breakdown

Definition 6.1 (UMVUE) An unbiased estimator \(\hat{\theta}^*\) is the Uniformly Minimum Variance Unbiased Estimator (UMVUE) if for any other unbiased estimator \(\tilde{\theta}\): \[ \text{Var}(\hat{\theta}^*) \le \text{Var}(\tilde{\theta}) \quad \forall \theta \in \Theta \] If an unbiased estimator achieves the Cramér-Rao Lower Bound with equality, it is guaranteed to be UMVUE.
Example 6.2 (Bernoulli Sample Mean is UMVUE) For \(X_1, \dots, X_n \sim \text{Bernoulli}(p)\), \(f(x; p) = p^x (1-p)^{1-x}\).
\(\ln f = x \ln p + (1-x) \ln(1-p)\). Differentiating: \[ \frac{\partial}{\partial p}\ln f = \frac{x}{p} - \frac{1-x}{1-p} = \frac{x - p}{p(1-p)} \] Squaring and taking expectation: \[ I_1(p) = E\left[\left(\frac{X - p}{p(1-p)}\right)^2\right] = \frac{\text{Var}(X)}{p^2(1-p)^2} = \frac{p(1-p)}{p^2(1-p)^2} = \frac{1}{p(1-p)} \] For \(n\) i.i.d. observations, \(I_n(p) = \frac{n}{p(1-p)}\). The CRLB for \(p\) is therefore: \[ \text{CRLB} = \frac{1}{I_n(p)} = \frac{p(1-p)}{n} \] The sample mean \(\bar{X}\) has variance \(\text{Var}(\bar{X}) = \frac{\text{Var}(X_i)}{n} = \frac{p(1-p)}{n}\). Because \(\text{Var}(\bar{X}) = \text{CRLB}\), \(\bar{X}\) achieves the lowest possible variance of any conceivable unbiased estimator. It is the UMVUE!
Regularity Condition Breakdown: Uniform\((0, \theta)\) For \(X_i \sim \text{Uniform}(0, \theta)\), the support \([0, \theta]\) depends directly on \(\theta\). The Leibniz rule for differentiating under the integral sign is invalid.

Indeed, the estimator \(\hat{\theta} = \frac{n+1}{n} X_{(n)}\) is unbiased, but its variance is \(\frac{\theta^2}{n(n+2)} = O(1/n^2)\). It converges substantially faster than the standard parametric rate \(O(1/n)\) prescribed by CRLB!
Summary Takeaway CRLB provides the ultimate benchmark for estimator variance.
