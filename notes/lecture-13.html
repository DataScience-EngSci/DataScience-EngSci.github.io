1. What is Artificial Intelligence?

Having developed classical probability models, maximum likelihood estimation, and hypothesis testing in Lectures 01 through 12, we now transition to the computational paradigm of modern data science: Artificial Intelligence (AI) and Machine Learning (ML).

Definition 1.1 (Artificial Intelligence) Artificial Intelligence is the branch of computer science dedicated to simulating human cognitive faculties in machines—including vision, auditory perception, speech, conceptual understanding, learning, and autonomous decision-making.
AI is inherently multidisciplinary, situated at the nexus of:
  • Cognitive Science: Modeling mental representation and thought processes.
  • Neuroscience: Understanding biological neural circuits and biological information processing.
  • Psychology & Linguistics: Studying behavioral adaptation and natural language syntax and semantics.
  • Mathematics, Statistics & Optimization: Providing the rigorous formal engine for learning from empirical observations.

The concrete operational goals of AI systems can be broken down into six foundational capabilities:

  1. Learning : Inferring underlying patterns, rules, or statistical distributions from empirical experience.
  2. Reasoning : Drawing valid logical or probabilistic inferences from knowledge bases and sensory inputs.
  3. Concept Understanding : Forming abstract representations that generalize across varied instances.
  4. Decision Making : Choosing optimal actions among alternatives to achieve defined goals under uncertainty.
  5. Prediction : Forecasting future outcomes, unobserved variables, or trend trajectories.
  6. Environmental Action : Executing actions through physical actuators or digital agents that modify the surrounding state.

2. Weak AI vs. Strong AI

Contemporary AI architectures are categorized by the scope and autonomy of their operational competence:

Taxonomy 2.1 (Weak AI vs. Strong AI)
  • Weak / Narrow AI :
    Systems engineered to solve specific, tightly-bounded tasks within defined domains. Rather than replacing human general cognition, they serve as assistive tools. Examples highlighted in class:
    • Voice assistants: Amazon Alexa, Apple Siri.
    • Game AI: Automated opponents and non-player characters in video games.
    • Filtering engines: Automated spam detection, targeted recommendation algorithms.
  • Strong / General AI :
    Autonomous systems possessing broad, robust intelligence capable of operating in safety-critical, complex, and unconstrained environments without human intervention. Examples highlighted in class:
    • Autonomous Vehicles: Real-time sensor fusion, path planning, hazard avoidance, and high-speed trajectory control.
    • Surgical AI / Autonomous Operating Rooms: Robotic microsurgery, dynamic tissue tracking, and sub-millimeter anatomical precision.

3. The Hierarchy: AI, Machine Learning, and Deep Learning

A foundational relationship connects Artificial Intelligence, Machine Learning, and Deep Learning as concentric sub-disciplines:

The Nested Paradigms of AI

1. Artificial Intelligence (Outer Layer)

"Computers making decisions with logic." Encompasses all rule-based expert systems, symbolic reasoning, heuristic search (such as \(A^*\)), and knowledge graphs.

2. Machine Learning (Middle Layer)

"Computers making decisions without being explicitly programmed." Algorithms whose internal decision parameters are automatically updated by statistical optimization over empirical datasets.

3. Deep Learning (Core Layer)

"Computers making decisions where decision criteria do not have to be manually defined or guided." Hierarchical representation learning using multi-layered artificial neural networks where features are learned end-to-end from raw data (pixels, raw waveforms, token sequences).

4. What is Machine Learning? Experience, Data, and Complexity

Following the formal characterization in statistical learning theory:

Definition 4.1 (Machine Learning) Machine learning consists of computational methods that leverage experience to systematically improve performance at a specific task or to make accurate, generalizable predictions on unobserved data.

In computational practice:

  • Experience (\(E\)): Refers to historical data made available to the learner, typically formatted as structured electronic datasets (digitized feature vectors, time series, or human-labeled supervision pairs).
  • Data Quality & Size: The statistical consistency and generalization capacity of any learner strictly depend on both the sample size \(n\) and the label quality (signal-to-noise ratio).
  • Document Classification Example: Consider classifying randomly sampled text documents into topic categories (e.g., politics, business, sports, weather). As the sample size \(n\) grows, the variance of the estimator shrinks; however, performance degrades if human labelers introduce misclassification noise, or if the cardinality of the topic space is large.

5. Computational Complexity vs. Sample Complexity

In classical theoretical computer science, algorithms are evaluated primarily along two resource dimensions: \[ \text{Time Complexity } \mathcal{O}(T(n)) \quad \text{and} \quad \text{Space Complexity } \mathcal{O}(S(n)) \] In statistical machine learning, however, an equally fundamental theoretical criterion is required:

Definition 5.1 (Sample Complexity) Sample Complexity evaluates the minimal number of training examples \(m = m(\epsilon, \delta)\) required by a learning algorithm to guarantee, with high probability (\(1 - \delta\)), that its generalization error is bounded above by \(\epsilon\): \[ P\left( R(h) - R^* \le \epsilon \right) \ge 1 - \delta \] Sample complexity depends fundamentally on:
  1. The richness / expressive capacity of the chosen concept class or hypothesis set \(\mathcal{H}\) (measured via VC-dimension or Rademacher complexity).
  2. The inherent noise level of the underlying data-generating distribution \(\mathcal{D}\).

7. Taxonomy of Learning Scenarios

The mechanism by which the learner interacts with information defines the learning paradigm:

  • Supervised Learning: Training instances are pairs \((x_i, y_i)\) drawn i.i.d. from distribution \(\mathcal{D}\). Given a new unseen feature vector \(x_{\text{new}}\), the goal is to predict its target label \(y_{\text{new}}\).
  • Unsupervised Learning: The learner receives only unlabeled observations \(\{x_i\}\) without target annotations (\(X, \bcancel{Y}\)). The objective is to discover latent geometric structure, probability density contours, or discrete clusters.
  • Semi-Supervised Learning: Leverages a small corpus of labeled examples alongside a vastly larger body of unlabeled data to guide the placement of decision boundaries through low-density regions.
  • Self-Supervised Learning: Supervision signals are generated autonomously from the structure of the input data itself (e.g., predicting masked tokens in BERT or contrasting image augmentations in SimCLR).
  • Ensemble Learning: Combines a collection of base hypotheses (via bagging, random forests, or gradient boosting) to produce a composite model with dramatically lower variance or bias.
  • Transfer Learning: Repurposing representations acquired on a data-rich source task (e.g., ImageNet pre-training) to achieve rapid convergence on a data-scarce target task.
  • Multi-Task Learning: Jointly training a model on multiple related tasks simultaneously, sharing internal representations to exploit inductive transfer across domains.
  • Adversarial Learning: Designing minimax training objectives (such as Generative Adversarial Networks) or hardening classifiers against worst-case adversarial input perturbations.
  • Online Learning: Sequential processing where training examples arrive stream-wise one at a time, requiring continual, low-latency hypothesis updates without storing the full history.
  • Reinforcement Learning: An autonomous agent interacts with a dynamic environment via a Markov Decision Process (MDP), taking actions \(a_t \in \mathcal{A}\), receiving state feedback \(s_{t+1}\), and maximizing expected cumulative discounted reward \(\sum \gamma^t r_t\).

8. Core Machine Learning Problem Types

The Canonical Problem Types
  • Classification: Mapping inputs to discrete categorical classes \(\mathcal{Y} = \{c_1, \dots, c_K\}\). There is generally no continuous metric distance defined between classes. Examples:
    • Document topic tagging (\(\{\text{politics}, \text{business}, \text{sports}, \text{weather}\}\)).
    • Object detection (\(\{\text{car}, \text{train}, \text{airplane}\}\)).
    • Binary botanical classification: *Iris-Virginica* vs. *Not Iris-Virginica*.
  • Regression: Predicting a continuous real-valued response \(y \in \mathbb{R}\). The loss penalty is a direct function of the numerical distance between the true value \(y\) and the prediction \(\hat{y}\) (e.g., squared error \((y - \hat{y})^2\)).
  • Ranking: Learning an ordering over a set of items based on a utility criterion. The canonical example is web search query result ranking.
  • Clustering: Unsupervised partitioning of unlabeled instances into homogeneous subgroups or natural communities without pre-assigned ground truth labels.
  • Dimensionality Reduction / Manifold Learning: Projecting high-dimensional observations \(\mathbb{R}^D\) into a compact representation \(\mathbb{R}^d\) (\(d \ll D\)) while preserving pairwise distances, topological neighborhood graphs, or maximal variance.

9. Mathematical Formalization of the Learning Stages

To formulate machine learning mathematically, we introduce the formal vocabulary:

Formal Mathematical Vocabulary
  • Instance / Example (\(x \in \mathcal{X}\)): An individual object or data point. In email filtering, this represents a single raw email message.
  • Feature Vector (\(\mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^d\)): An ordered vector of numerical measurements extracted from an instance: \[ \mathbf{x} = \begin{bmatrix} x_1 & x_2 & \dots & x_d \end{bmatrix}^T \] E.g., message length, sender domain reputation score, keyword frequencies.
  • Label (\(y \in \mathcal{Y}\)): The ground truth target. \(\mathcal{Y} = \{-1, +1\}\) for binary spam classification; \(\mathcal{Y} = \mathbb{R}\) for regression.
  • Hypothesis Set (\(\mathcal{H}\)): A defined class of candidate functions mapping feature vectors to labels: \[ \mathcal{H} = \{ h_\theta : \mathcal{X} \to \mathcal{Y} \mid \theta \in \Theta \} \]
  • Loss Function (\(L\)): A non-negative mapping measuring prediction error: \[ L: \mathcal{Y} \times \mathcal{Y} \to \mathbb{R}_+ \] Common instances include \(0\text{--}1\) loss \(L(y, \hat{y}) = \mathbf{1}_{\{y \ne \hat{y}\}}\) and squared error \(L(y, \hat{y}) = (y - \hat{y})^2\).
  • Hyperparameters: Structural configuration parameters (regularization weight \(\lambda\), polynomial degree \(p\), tree depth) that are chosen prior to running optimization, rather than learned directly by gradient descent.

10. Dataset Partitioning, Validation & Generalization Error

A fundamental axiom of machine learning is that training error is an overly optimistic, biased estimator of true test error. To ensure rigorous, unbiased evaluation, empirical data must be partitioned into three mutually disjoint subsets:

The Three-Way Data Partition
  1. Training Sample (\(S_{\text{train}}\)): Used by algorithm \(\mathcal{A}(\Theta)\) to optimize the model's primary internal parameters \(\theta\) (e.g., weights in regression or neural network weights).
  2. Validation Sample (\(S_{\text{val}}\)): Used to evaluate performance across different hyperparameter choices \(\Theta_0\) (e.g., tuning regularization parameter \(\lambda\) or model complexity) to prevent overfitting.
  3. Test Sample (\(S_{\text{test}}\)): Held out strictly until the final model is selected. Evaluated once via the loss function to produce an unbiased estimate of future generalization performance.
The Generalization Principle:
The ultimate success of any machine learning system is measured strictly by its generalization error on unobserved test data drawn from the true underlying distribution \(\mathcal{D}\), never by its ability to memorize the training sample.

11. Visual Geometry: Continuous Regression vs. Discrete Classification

The lecture concludes with a direct geometric contrast between the two core supervised tasks:

Geometric Comparison
1. Continuous Regression:

Given continuous coordinate points \((x_i, y_i)\), find the optimal function (e.g. line \(\hat{y} = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x\)) that minimizes vertical distance residuals: \[ \min_{\beta_0, \beta_1} \sum_{i=1}^n (y_i - (\beta_0 + \beta_1 x_i))^2 \] The output is a real-valued number along a continuum.

2. Discrete Classification:

Given feature coordinates (e.g., Petal Length vs. Petal Width in Fisher's Iris data), find a separating decision boundary (such as the linear hyper-plane dashed line) that partitions the feature space into discrete class regions: \[ \hat{y} = \text{sign}(\mathbf{w}^T \mathbf{x} + b) \] Assigning instances to either Iris-Virginica or Not Iris-Virginica.
