1. Motivation: What is Statistical Inference?

The overarching goal of Data Science and Machine Learning is to make rigorous assertions about complex phenomena from limited observations. Statistical inference is the formal mathematical process of learning about unknown characteristics of an entire population based solely on what is observed in a relatively small sample collected from that population.

Motivating Example (Sockeye Salmon in the Pacific Northwest)

Suppose an ecological research team wants to determine the true average weight (\(\mu\)) of all adult Sockeye salmon inhabiting a specific river basin in the Pacific Northwest of the United States.

  • We cannot capture and weigh millions of wild salmon in the entire population without destroying the ecosystem and incurring astronomical costs.
  • Instead, we collect a random sample of \(n\) salmon and compute their sample average: \[ \bar{X} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n X_i \]
  • Key Scientific Questions: Assuming our sampling procedure is truly random and unbiased, will \(\bar{X}\) be close to the true population average \(\mu\)? How confident can we be?
  • Governing Factors: The answer depends directly on the sample size \(n\) and the intrinsic variability (variance \(\sigma^2\)) of weights across the population.

While estimating a population mean using a sample average is intuitively obvious, many real-world parameters governing population mechanisms (such as scale parameters, truncation thresholds, or mixture proportions) are abstract and lack trivial sample counterparts. The theoretical toolkit of probability allows us to formalize what makes an estimator "good" (unbiased, consistent, efficient) and derive optimal estimators in non-obvious scenarios.

2. Probability Foundations & Kolmogorov's Axioms

To model non-deterministic experiments systematically, we define three foundational building blocks:

Definition 2.1 (Sample Space & Events)
  • Sample Space (\(\Omega\)): The set of all possible fundamental outcomes of a random experiment.
  • Event (\(A\)): Any subset of the sample space (\(A \subseteq \Omega\)). An event occurs if the realized experimental outcome \(\omega \in \Omega\) lies in \(A\).
  • Event Space (\(\mathcal{F}\)): The family of all considered events \(\{A_1, A_2, \dots, A_n\}\) forming a \(\sigma\)-algebra over \(\Omega\).
Axioms 2.2 (Kolmogorov's Three Probability Axioms) A probability measure \(P: \mathcal{F} \to [0, 1]\) assigns a real-valued likelihood to each event satisfying:
  1. Non-negativity: For any event \(A \subseteq \Omega\): \[ P(A) \ge 0 \]
  2. Normalization: The probability of the certain event is unity: \[ P(\Omega) = 1 \]
  3. Countable Additivity (Disjoint Events): If a sequence of events \(\{A_i\}\) are pairwise disjoint (mutually exclusive, \(A_i \cap A_j = \emptyset\) for all \(i \neq j\)), then: \[ P\left(\bigcup_{i=1}^n A_i\right) = \sum_{i=1}^n P(A_i) \] In particular, if \(\{A_1, \dots, A_n\}\) forms a complete partition of \(\Omega\), then \(\sum_{i=1}^n P(A_i) = P(\Omega) = 1\).
Example 2.3 (Rolling a Fair Six-Sided Die & Frequentist Interpretation)

Consider the classical random experiment of rolling a balanced die: \[ \Omega = \{1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6\} \] The impossible event has zero probability: \(P(\emptyset) = 0\), while the sample space satisfies \(P(\Omega) = 1\).

  • Event \(A = \text{"roll an even number"} = \{2, 4, 6\}\): \[ P(A) = \frac{|\{2, 4, 6\}|}{|\Omega|} = \frac{3}{6} = \frac{1}{2} \]
  • Event \(B = \{1, 2, 3\}\): \[ P(B) = \frac{3}{6} = \frac{1}{2} \]

Frequentist Interpretation: If the physical experiment is repeated \(n\) independent times and event \(A\) is observed \(n_A\) times, the probability \(P(A)\) represents the limiting relative frequency as the number of trials tends to infinity: \[ P(A) = \lim_{n \to \infty} \frac{n_A}{n} = \frac{1}{2} \]

3. Set Operations, Venn Diagrams & Fundamental Bounds

From Kolmogorov's axioms, several essential geometric set properties and probability inequalities follow directly:

Proposition 3.1 (Core Set Identities & Probability Bounds) For any events \(A, B \subseteq \Omega\):
  1. Monotonicity: If \(A \subseteq B\), then the occurrence of \(A\) guarantees the occurrence of \(B\): \[ A \subseteq B \implies P(A) \le P(B) \]
  2. Intersection Bound: Since \(A \cap B \subseteq A\) and \(A \cap B \subseteq B\): \[ P(A \cap B) \le \min\big(P(A), P(B)\big) \]
  3. Union Bound (Boole's Inequality): For arbitrary overlapping events: \[ P(A \cup B) \le P(A) + P(B) \] with equality holding if and only if \(A\) and \(B\) are mutually disjoint (\(A \cap B = \emptyset\)).
  4. Complement Rule: The probability that event \(A\) does not occur (\(\Omega \setminus A = A^c\)) is: \[ P(\Omega \setminus A) = 1 - P(A) \]

4. Conditional Probability & Bayes' Theorem

In statistical modeling, we rarely observe phenomena in isolation; rather, we condition our beliefs on incoming evidence.

Definition 4.1 (Conditional Probability & Product Rule) The conditional probability of event \(A\) given that event \(B\) has occurred (with \(P(B) > 0\)) is defined as: \[ P(A \mid B) \triangleq \frac{P(A \cap B)}{P(B)} \] Multiplying both sides by the denominator yields the symmetrical Product Rule: \[ P(A \cap B) = P(A \mid B) P(B) = P(B \mid A) P(A) \]
Theorem 4.2 (Bayes' Theorem) Equating the two formulations of the joint probability \(P(A \cap B)\) yields Bayes' Rule: \[ P(B \mid A) = \frac{P(A \mid B) P(B)}{P(A)} \] where:
  • \(P(B)\) is the Prior Probability of hypothesis \(B\) before observing evidence \(A\).
  • \(P(A \mid B)\) is the Likelihood of observing evidence \(A\) under hypothesis \(B\).
  • \(P(B \mid A)\) is the Posterior Probability of hypothesis \(B\) updated in light of evidence \(A\).
  • \(P(A)\) is the total marginal probability (evidence).
Lecture Example (Digital Communications Channel Symbol Detection)

Consider a transmitter \(T\) sending one of \(n\) digital symbols \(\{\text{Symbol } 1, \text{Symbol } 2, \dots, \text{Symbol } n\}\) across a noisy physical channel to a receiver \(R\). Suppose the receiver observes an analogue voltage signal \(R_1\).

To perform optimal symbol detection, the receiver must calculate the posterior decoding probability that Symbol 1 was transmitted, conditioned on observing signal \(R_1\): \[ P(\text{Symbol } 1 \mid R_1) = \frac{P(R_1 \mid \text{Symbol } 1) \, P(\text{Symbol } 1)}{P(R_1)} \] Here, \(P(\text{Symbol } 1)\) is the prior transmission frequency, \(P(R_1 \mid \text{Symbol } 1)\) describes the channel noise transition density, and \(P(\text{Symbol } 1 \mid R_1)\) allows the receiver to make a Minimum Error Rate decision rule.

5. Random Variables: Discrete vs. Continuous

Manipulating raw outcomes \(\omega\) in abstract sample spaces \(\Omega\) is cumbersome for mathematical computation. A random variable translates abstract experimental outcomes into real numbers.

Definition 5.1 (Random Variable) A random variable \(X\) is a real-valued function defined on the sample space: \[ X: \Omega \to \mathbb{R} \] For each realized experimental outcome \(\omega \in \Omega\), \(X(\omega)\) assigns a deterministic real number.

As emphasized in the lecture, random variables fall into two fundamental classes based on the cardinality of their support:

Discrete vs. Continuous Distinction (Slide Examples)
  • Discrete Random Variable: Takes on values in a countable set \(\text{Val}(X) \subseteq \mathbb{R}\).
    Slide Example: In \(10\) independent tosses of a coin, let \(X\) be the number of heads obtained: \[ X(\omega) \in \{0, 1, 2, \dots, 10\} \] The probability of observing exactly \(k\) heads is the measure of the underlying pre-image: \[ P(X = k) = P\big(\{\omega \in \Omega : X(\omega) = k\}\big) \]
  • Continuous Random Variable: Takes on an uncountable continuum of possible values (such as an interval in \(\mathbb{R}\)).
    Slide Example: Let \(X\) measure the exact duration of time elapsed until an unstable radioactive particle decays: \[ X(\omega) \in [0, \infty) \] For continuous variables, probabilities are evaluated over intervals rather than isolated points: \[ P(a \le X \le b) = P\big(\{\omega \in \Omega : a \le X(\omega) \le b\}\big) \]

6. Probability Mass Function (PMF) & Cumulative Distribution Function (CDF)

The Probability Mass Function (PMF)

For a discrete random variable \(X\), the distribution of probability is fully characterized by its Probability Mass Function, denoted \(p_X(x)\):

Definition 6.1 (PMF Properties) \[ p_X(x) \triangleq P(X = x) \] satisfying:
  1. \(0 \le p_X(x) \le 1\) for all \(x \in \text{Val}(X)\).
  2. Total probability normalization: \[ \sum_{x \in \text{Val}(X)} p_X(x) = 1 \]
  3. For any subset of values \(A \subseteq \text{Val}(X)\): \[ P(X \in A) = \sum_{x \in A} p_X(x) \]
Slide Diagram (Discrete Probability Spikes)

Consider a discrete random variable with support \(\text{Val}(X) = \{1, 2, 3, 4\}\) with corresponding mass values \(p_1, p_2, p_3, p_4\). The normalization condition requires: \[ p_1 + p_2 + p_3 + p_4 = 1 \] To find the probability that \(X\) takes an even value, we simply sum the discrete spikes: \[ P(X \text{ is even}) = P(X \in \{2, 4\}) = p_2 + p_4 \]

The Cumulative Distribution Function (CDF)

The Cumulative Distribution Function (CDF), denoted \(F_X(x)\), provides a universal description valid for both discrete and continuous random variables:

Definition 6.2 (Cumulative Distribution Function - CDF) \[ F_X(x) \triangleq P(X \le x) \] Properties of any valid CDF:
  1. Boundedness: \(0 \le F_X(x) \le 1\) for all \(x \in \mathbb{R}\).
  2. Asymptotic Limits: \[ \lim_{x \to -\infty} F_X(x) = 0 \quad \text{and} \quad \lim_{x \to +\infty} F_X(x) = 1 \]
  3. Monotonicity: The CDF is non-decreasing: \[ x \le y \implies F_X(x) \le F_X(y) \]

7. Probability Density Function (PDF) & The \(f_X(x) \neq P(X=x)\) Caveat

For a continuous random variable, the CDF \(F_X(x)\) is continuous and differentiable. Its derivative defines the Probability Density Function (PDF):

Definition 7.1 (Probability Density Function - PDF) \[ f_X(x) \triangleq \frac{d F_X(x)}{dx} = \lim_{\Delta x \to 0} \frac{F_X(x + \Delta x) - F_X(x)}{\Delta x} \] Rearranging this infinitesimal limit gives the standard physical interpretation of probability density: \[ P(x \le X < x + \Delta x) \approx f_X(x) \, \Delta x \]
Crucial Theoretical Warning (Circled in Red on Slides) \[ \mathbf{f_X(x) \neq P(X = x)} \]

Students frequently conflate probability density with point probability. In continuous distributions:

  • Point Probability is Zero: The probability of a continuous random variable taking on any single exact real number is precisely zero: \[ P(X = x) = \int_x^x f_X(t) \, dt = 0 \]
  • Densities Can Exceed Unity: While probabilities are strictly capped at \(1\), the density \(f_X(x)\) represents probability per unit length and can take arbitrary non-negative values. For example, if \(X \sim \text{Uniform}(0, 0.1)\), then \(f_X(x) = 10\) for all \(x \in (0, 0.1)\).
Proposition 7.2 (Integration Properties of the PDF) Every valid continuous PDF satisfies:
  1. Non-negativity: \(f_X(x) \ge 0\) for all \(x \in \mathbb{R}\).
  2. Normalization: The total area under the density curve equals \(1\): \[ \int_{-\infty}^\infty f_X(x) \, dx = 1 \]
  3. Recovery of the CDF: \[ F_X(x) = \int_{-\infty}^x f_X(x') \, dx' \]
  4. Interval Probabilities: For any interval \([a, b]\): \[ P(a \le X \le b) = \int_a^b f_X(x) \, dx \] Slide Illustration: For instance, \(P(1 \le X \le 2) = \int_1^2 f_X(x) \, dx\), representing the shaded area under the bell curve between \(x = 1\) and \(x = 2\).
