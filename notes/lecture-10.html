1. The Role of Asymptotics in Statistical Inference

In finite samples, finding estimators that are strictly unbiased or exactly achieve the Cramér-Rao Lower Bound is often analytically intractable. In big data regimes, however, the sample size \(n\) is large (\(n \to \infty\)). Asymptotic theory analyzes the limiting behavior of estimators as data accumulates indefinitely.

2. The Four Golden Asymptotic Properties of MLE

Under standard Cramér regularity conditions (differentiable log-likelihood, non-boundary true parameter \(\theta_0 \in \text{int}(\Theta)\), support independent of \(\theta\), and strictly positive Fisher Information \(I_1(\theta_0) > 0\)), the Maximum Likelihood Estimator \(\hat{\theta}_n\) enjoys four extraordinary theoretical guarantees:

Theorem 2.1 (The Four Asymptotic Properties of MLE)
  1. Consistency: The MLE converges in probability to the true parameter value: \[ \hat{\theta}_n \overset{P}{\longrightarrow} \theta_0 \quad \text{as } n \to \infty \]
  2. Asymptotic Unbiasedness: Even if biased in finite samples (such as the \(\frac{n-1}{n}\) factor in Gaussian variance), the expected value converges to the truth: \[ \lim_{n \to \infty} E\left[\hat{\theta}_n\right] = \theta_0 \]
  3. Asymptotic Efficiency: The variance of the MLE converges to the Cramér-Rao Lower Bound with equality: \[ \lim_{n \to \infty} \frac{\text{CRLB}_n(\theta_0)}{\text{Var}\left(\hat{\theta}_n\right)} = 1 \] No consistent estimator can possess smaller asymptotic variance!
  4. Asymptotic Normality: The sampling distribution of the MLE approaches a Gaussian distribution centered at \(\theta_0\): \[ \sqrt{n}\left(\hat{\theta}_n - \theta_0\right) \overset{d}{\longrightarrow} \mathcal{N}\left(0, \frac{1}{I_1(\theta_0)}\right) \] Equivalently: \[ \frac{\hat{\theta}_n - \theta_0}{\sqrt{\text{CRLB}_n(\theta_0)}} \overset{d}{\longrightarrow} \mathcal{N}(0, 1) \]

3. Continuous Mapping Theorem & Consistency Proofs

To rigorously establish the consistency of non-linear estimators, we use fundamental algebraic properties and the Continuous Mapping Theorem for convergence in probability.

Theorem 3.1 (Algebraic Properties of Convergence in Probability) Suppose \(\{X_n\}\) and \(\{Y_n\}\) are sequences of random variables such that \(X_n \overset{P}{\longrightarrow} X\) and \(Y_n \overset{P}{\longrightarrow} Y\). Then:
  • Sum rule: \(X_n + Y_n \overset{P}{\longrightarrow} X + Y\)
  • Product rule: \(X_n Y_n \overset{P}{\longrightarrow} X Y\)
  • Quotient rule: \(\frac{X_n}{Y_n} \overset{P}{\longrightarrow} \frac{X}{Y}\)  (provided \(P(Y \neq 0) = 1\))
  • Continuous Mapping Theorem (CMT): \(g(X_n) \overset{P}{\longrightarrow} g(X)\) for any function \(g\) continuous on the support of \(X\).
Application: Consistency of the Exponential Rate MLE Let \(X_1, \dots, X_n \overset{\text{i.i.d.}}{\sim} \text{Exponential}(\lambda)\). In Lecture 05, we found the MLE to be \(\hat{\lambda}_n = \frac{1}{\bar{X}_n}\).

Step 1 (WLLN): By the Weak Law of Large Numbers, the sample mean converges in probability to the population mean:

\[ \bar{X}_n \overset{P}{\longrightarrow} E[X_1] = \frac{1}{\lambda} \]

Step 2 (Continuous Mapping): Is it true that \(\hat{\lambda}_n = \frac{1}{\bar{X}_n} \overset{P}{\longrightarrow} \frac{1}{1/\lambda} = \lambda\)? Yes! Since \(\lambda > 0\), the transformation \(g(x) = \frac{1}{x}\) is continuous at \(x = \frac{1}{\lambda} > 0\). Applying the Continuous Mapping Theorem:

\[ \hat{\lambda}_n = g(\bar{X}_n) = \frac{1}{\bar{X}_n} \overset{P}{\longrightarrow} g\left(\frac{1}{\lambda}\right) = \frac{1}{1/\lambda} = \lambda \]

Thus, \(\hat{\lambda}_n\) is a consistent estimator of \(\lambda\). ✔

4. Finite-Sample vs. Asymptotic Behavior: Exponential MLE

Let us rigorously verify how finite-sample bias disappears and asymptotic efficiency is attained for the Exponential rate estimator.

Exact Sampling Distribution and Moments The sum \(S_n = \sum_{i=1}^n X_i\) follows a \(\text{Gamma}(n, \lambda)\) distribution with density: \[ f_{S_n}(s) = \frac{\lambda^n}{\Gamma(n)} s^{n-1} e^{-\lambda s}, \quad s > 0 \] The MLE is \(\hat{\lambda}_n = \frac{n}{S_n}\). Its expectation is: \[ E\left[\hat{\lambda}_n\right] = n E\left[\frac{1}{S_n}\right] = n \int_0^\infty \frac{1}{s} \frac{\lambda^n}{(n-1)!} s^{n-1} e^{-\lambda s} \, ds = \frac{n \lambda^n}{(n-1)!} \int_0^\infty s^{n-2} e^{-\lambda s} \, ds \] Recognizing the standard Gamma integral \(\int_0^\infty s^{n-2} e^{-\lambda s} \, ds = \frac{(n-2)!}{\lambda^{n-1}}\): \[ E\left[\hat{\lambda}_n\right] = \frac{n \lambda^n}{(n-1)!} \frac{(n-2)!}{\lambda^{n-1}} = \frac{n}{n - 1}\lambda \] Notice that in finite samples, \(\hat{\lambda}_n\) is biased upward: \(\text{Bias} = \frac{\lambda}{n-1} > 0\). However: \[ \lim_{n \to \infty} E\left[\hat{\lambda}_n\right] = \lim_{n \to \infty} \left(\frac{n}{n - 1}\right)\lambda = \lambda \quad (\text{Asymptotically Unbiased}) \] Similarly, computing the second moment yields: \[ E\left[\hat{\lambda}_n^2\right] = \frac{n^2}{(n - 1)(n - 2)}\lambda^2 \implies \text{Var}\left(\hat{\lambda}_n\right) = \frac{n^2}{(n - 1)^2 (n - 2)}\lambda^2 \] Recall from Lecture 08 that \(\text{CRLB}_n(\lambda) = \frac{\lambda^2}{n}\). Now compute the efficiency ratio: \[ \lim_{n \to \infty} \frac{\text{CRLB}_n(\lambda)}{\text{Var}\left(\hat{\lambda}_n\right)} = \lim_{n \to \infty} \frac{\frac{\lambda^2}{n}}{\frac{n^2}{(n-1)^2(n-2)}\lambda^2} = \lim_{n \to \infty} \frac{(n - 1)^2 (n - 2)}{n^3} = 1 \quad \blacksquare \]

5. General Sufficient Conditions for Consistency

Theorem 5.1 (MSE-Based Consistency) Let \(\hat{\theta}_n\) be any sequence of estimators for parameter \(\theta\). If:
  1. \(\lim_{n \to \infty} \text{Bias}\left(\hat{\theta}_n\right) = 0\) (Asymptotically unbiased)
  2. \(\lim_{n \to \infty} \text{Var}\left(\hat{\theta}_n\right) = 0\) (Variance vanishes)
Then \(\lim_{n \to \infty} \text{MSE}\left(\hat{\theta}_n\right) = 0\), which guarantees: \[ \hat{\theta}_n \overset{P}{\longrightarrow} \theta \quad (\text{Consistency}) \]
Proof By Chebyshev's inequality applied to \(\hat{\theta}_n\): \[ P(|\hat{\theta}_n - \theta| \ge \varepsilon) \le \frac{E\left[(\hat{\theta}_n - \theta)^2\right]}{\varepsilon^2} = \frac{\text{MSE}\left(\hat{\theta}_n\right)}{\varepsilon^2} = \frac{\text{Var}(\hat{\theta}_n) + [\text{Bias}(\hat{\theta}_n)]^2}{\varepsilon^2} \] Taking \(n \to \infty\), both variance and bias squared vanish, driving the bound to \(0\). \(\blacksquare\)

6. Non-Regular Case: Uniform Distribution Order Statistics

To see the power of the variance-vanishing condition, consider the non-regular uniform model where standard derivative-based likelihood theory fails because the support depends on the parameter.

Example: Maximum Estimator for Uniform\((0, \theta)\)

Let \(X_1, X_2, \dots, X_n \overset{\text{i.i.d.}}{\sim} \text{Uniform}(0, \theta)\). The maximum order statistic is:

\[ Y_n = \max(X_1, X_2, \dots, X_n) \]

Is \(Y_n\) a consistent estimator of \(\theta\)? Let us derive its exact sampling distribution:

Step 1: Cumulative Distribution Function (CDF):

\[ \begin{aligned} F_{Y_n}(y) = P(Y_n \le y) &= P(\max(X_1, \dots, X_n) \le y) \\ &= P(X_1 \le y, X_2 \le y, \dots, X_n \le y) \\ &= P(X_1 \le y) \cdot P(X_2 \le y) \cdots P(X_n \le y) \quad (\text{by independence}) \\ &= [P(X_1 \le y)]^n = \left[\frac{y}{\theta}\right]^n, \quad 0 \le y \le \theta \end{aligned} \]

Step 2: Probability Density Function (PDF): Differentiating the CDF with respect to \(y\):

\[ f_{Y_n}(y) = \frac{d}{dy} F_{Y_n}(y) = \frac{d}{dy}\left[\frac{y}{\theta}\right]^n = \frac{n}{\theta^n} y^{n-1}, \quad 0 \le y \le \theta \]

Step 3: Expected Value (Mean):

\[ E[Y_n] = \int_{-\infty}^\infty y f_{Y_n}(y) \, dy = \int_0^\theta y \left(\frac{n}{\theta^n} y^{n-1}\right) dy = \frac{n}{\theta^n} \int_0^\theta y^n \, dy = \frac{n}{\theta^n} \left[\frac{y^{n+1}}{n+1}\right]_0^\theta = \frac{n}{n+1} \theta \]

Notice that \(\lim_{n \to \infty} E[Y_n] = \lim_{n \to \infty} \frac{n}{n+1}\theta = \theta\), so \(Y_n\) is asymptotically unbiased.

Step 4: Variance and Consistency: Computing the second moment:

\[ E[Y_n^2] = \int_0^\theta y^2 \left(\frac{n}{\theta^n} y^{n-1}\right) dy = \frac{n}{\theta^n} \int_0^\theta y^{n+1} \, dy = \frac{n}{n+2} \theta^2 \] \[ \text{Var}(Y_n) = E[Y_n^2] - (E[Y_n])^2 = \frac{n}{n+2}\theta^2 - \left(\frac{n}{n+1}\right)^2 \theta^2 = \frac{n}{(n+1)^2(n+2)} \theta^2 \]

As \(n \to \infty\):

\[ \lim_{n \to \infty} \text{Var}(Y_n) = \lim_{n \to \infty} \frac{n}{(n+1)^2(n+2)}\theta^2 = 0 \]

Since \(Y_n\) is asymptotically unbiased and its variance converges to \(0\), it satisfies the Chebyshev / generalized Markov condition:

\[ Y_n = \max(X_1, \dots, X_n) \overset{P}{\longrightarrow} \theta \quad \text{Consistent!} \quad \text{\checkmark} \]
Pedagogical Insight: Notice that \(\text{Var}(Y_n) \sim \frac{\theta^2}{n^2}\), decaying at rate \(O(1/n^2)\) rather than the standard parametric rate \(O(1/n)\). The maximum approaches the boundary \(\theta\) much faster than sample means converge to expectations!
