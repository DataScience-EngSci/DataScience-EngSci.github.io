1. The Philosophical Shift: Point Estimates vs. Interval Estimation

In Lectures 05 through 10, our primary objective was finding optimal point estimators \(\hat{\theta}(X_1, \dots, X_n)\) (such as the Maximum Likelihood Estimator \(\hat{\theta}_{\text{MLE}}\) or the Method of Moments estimator \(\hat{\theta}_{\text{MM}}\)). While point estimators possess asymptotic optimality (consistency, asymptotic efficiency attaining the CRLB), a single point estimate suffers from two fundamental limitations in practical data science:

  1. Zero Probability of Exact Hits: For any continuous parameter \(\theta \in \mathbb{R}\), the probability that a sample statistic coincides exactly with the ground truth is strictly zero: \[ P(\hat{\theta} = \theta) = 0 \]
  2. Absence of Uncertainty Quantification: Saying \(\hat{\mu} = 10.4\) gives no indication whether the true mean likely lies in \([10.2, 10.6]\) (high precision) or \([-5.0, 25.0]\) (pure noise).

To quantify precision, interval estimation seeks two sample statistics \(L(X_1, \dots, X_n)\) and \(U(X_1, \dots, X_n)\) defining a random interval \([L, U]\) that captures \(\theta\) with a pre-specified high probability \(1 - \alpha\).

Definition 1.1 (Confidence Interval) Let \(X_1, \dots, X_n\) be a sample drawn from a distribution indexed by \(\theta\). An interval \([L, U]\) computed from the sample is a \(100(1-\alpha)\%\) Confidence Interval for \(\theta\) if: \[ P\left(L(X_1, \dots, X_n) \le \theta \le U(X_1, \dots, X_n)\right) = 1 - \alpha \] The scalar \(1 - \alpha\) is the confidence level (typically \(90\%\), \(95\%\), or \(99\%\)), and \(\alpha \in (0, 1)\) is the significance level.
The Frequentist Meaning of Confidence (Slide 11.1, Slide 3): Suppose after collecting data, a calculated \(95\%\) confidence interval for \(\mu\) is \((-2.14, 3.07)\).

Common Fallacy: "There is a 95% probability that the parameter \(\mu\) lies between \(-2.14\) and \(3.07\)." — WRONG!

Correct Frequentist Interpretation: In classical statistics, the true parameter \(\mu\) is a fixed, non-random constant. The numbers \(-2.14\) and \(3.07\) are also fixed realized values. The statement \(-2.14 \le \mu \le 3.07\) is either entirely TRUE or entirely FALSE; there is no remaining randomness once data are observed.

The \(95\%\) probability refers to the random procedure \([L(X), U(X)]\) across hypothetically repeated samples. If 100 independent researchers each draw a random sample of size \(n\) from the same population and compute their own interval \([L, U]\), approximately 95 of those 100 intervals will cover \(\mu\), while approximately 5 will fail to cover it.

2. Exact Pivoting for Normal Mean with Known Variance (\(\sigma^2\) Known)

Consider an i.i.d. sample \(X_1, \dots, X_n \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2)\) where \(\sigma^2\) is known. The sample mean \(\bar{X} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n X_i\) is distributed as: \[ \bar{X} \sim \mathcal{N}\left(\mu, \frac{\sigma^2}{n}\right) \] We standardize \(\bar{X}\) into a pivotal quantity \(Z\): \[ Z = \frac{\bar{X} - \mu}{\sigma / \sqrt{n}} \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1) \] A pivotal quantity possesses the defining trait that its sampling distribution is completely known and free of all unknown parameters (here, \(\mathcal{N}(0, 1)\) does not depend on \(\mu\)).

Theorem 2.1 (The Pivoting Method) Let \(z_{\alpha/2}\) be the upper critical value of the standard normal distribution such that \(P(Z > z_{\alpha/2}) = \alpha/2\). By symmetry, \(P(Z < -z_{\alpha/2}) = \alpha/2\). Therefore: \[ P\left(-z_{\alpha/2} \le \frac{\bar{X} - \mu}{\sigma/\sqrt{n}} \le z_{\alpha/2}\right) = 1 - \alpha \] To isolate the parameter \(\mu\) "in the middle":
  1. Multiply through by the standard error \(\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}} > 0\): \[ -z_{\alpha/2}\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}} \le \bar{X} - \mu \le z_{\alpha/2}\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}} \]
  2. Subtract \(\bar{X}\) from all parts: \[ -\bar{X} - z_{\alpha/2}\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}} \le -\mu \le -\bar{X} + z_{\alpha/2}\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}} \]
  3. Multiply by \(-1\) (reversing the inequality signs): \[ \bar{X} - z_{\alpha/2}\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}} \le \mu \le \bar{X} + z_{\alpha/2}\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}} \]
Hence, the exact two-sided \(100(1-\alpha)\%\) Confidence Interval is: \[ \bar{X} \pm z_{\alpha/2} \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}} \]

3. Large-Sample Asymptotics & Worked Supermarket Produce Example

What if the underlying population is not normal, but the sample size \(n\) is large (\(n \ge 30\))? By the Central Limit Theorem (CLT): \[ \frac{\bar{X} - \mu}{\sigma/\sqrt{n}} \xrightarrow{d} \mathcal{N}(0, 1) \quad \text{as } n \to \infty \] Hence, regardless of population skewness or shape, the same interval \(\bar{X} \pm z_{\alpha/2}\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}\) provides an asymptotically valid confidence interval.

Worked Slide Example 11.1: Supermarket Produce Spending Problem Statement: A regional supermarket chain conducts a survey to evaluate customer spending on organic produce. A random sample of \(n = 200\) customers yields an average monthly spending of \(\bar{x} = \$36\). From historical market records, the population variance is assumed known at \(\sigma^2 = 5\) (hence \(\sigma = \sqrt{5} \approx 2.236\)).

Find an approximate \(90\%\) confidence interval for the true mean customer expenditure \(\mu\).

Step-by-Step Solution:
  1. Identify parameters: \[ n = 200, \quad \bar{x} = 36, \quad \sigma^2 = 5 \implies \sigma = \sqrt{5}, \quad 1 - \alpha = 0.90 \implies \alpha = 0.10, \quad \frac{\alpha}{2} = 0.05 \]
  2. Find critical value \(z_{0.05}\): We require \(z_{0.05}\) such that \(P(Z \le z_{0.05}) = 0.95\). In R: 
    > qnorm(0.95)
[1] 1.644854  # rounded to 1.645
  3. Compute Margin of Error: \[ E = z_{\alpha/2} \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}} = 1.645 \cdot \frac{\sqrt{5}}{\sqrt{200}} = 1.645 \cdot \frac{\sqrt{5}}{10\sqrt{2}} = 1.645 \cdot \frac{\sqrt{2.5}}{10} \approx 1.645 \cdot 0.15811 = 0.2601 \]
  4. Interval Bounds: \[ \bar{x} \pm E = 36 \pm 0.2601 \implies (35.74, \; 36.26) \]
Conclusion: We are \(90\%\) confident that the regional population average monthly expenditure on organic produce lies between \(\$35.74\) and \(\$36.26\).

4. Large Samples with Unknown Variance: Slutsky & Sample Standard Deviation

In nearly all practical engineering and data science applications, the population variance \(\sigma^2\) is unknown. We estimate \(\sigma^2\) using the unbiased sample variance: \[ S^2 = \frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{i=1}^n (X_i - \bar{X})^2 = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^n X_i^2 - \frac{1}{n}\left(\sum_{i=1}^n X_i\right)^2}{n-1} \] By the Weak Law of Large Numbers (WLLN), \(S^2 \xrightarrow{P} \sigma^2\), and by the Continuous Mapping Theorem: \[ S = \sqrt{S^2} \xrightarrow{P} \sigma \implies \frac{S}{\sigma} \xrightarrow{P} 1 \]

Theorem 4.1 (Slutsky's Asymptotic Justification for Large Samples) When replacing the unknown parameter \(\sigma\) with the random statistic \(S\), the standardized ratio decomposes into: \[ \frac{\bar{X} - \mu}{S/\sqrt{n}} = \underbrace{\left(\frac{\bar{X} - \mu}{\sigma/\sqrt{n}}\right)}_{\xrightarrow{d} \mathcal{N}(0, 1) \text{ by CLT}} \cdot \underbrace{\left(\frac{\sigma}{S}\right)}_{\xrightarrow{P} 1 \text{ by WLLN}} \xrightarrow{d} \mathcal{N}(0, 1) \] Therefore, for large samples (\(n > 30\)), an approximate \(100(1-\alpha)\%\) confidence interval for \(\mu\) under any underlying population distribution with finite variance is: \[ \bar{X} \pm z_{\alpha/2} \frac{S}{\sqrt{n}} \]

5. The Small-Sample Dilemma (\(n \le 30\)) & Why Normal Approximations Fail

What happens if the sample size is small (\(n \le 30\)) and \(\sigma^2\) is unknown?

The Fundamental Slide 11.2 Question: Can we use the normal approximation \(\bar{X} \pm z_{\alpha/2}\frac{S}{\sqrt{n}}\) for arbitrary distributions with \(n \le 30\)?

NO!

Two catastrophic failures occur simultaneously:
  1. The Central Limit Theorem has not converged: \(\bar{X}\) is not normal if the parent population is skewed or multimodal.
  2. The sample standard deviation \(S\) is random (as emphasized on slide 10). The variability of \(S\) is huge for small \(n\). In a small sample, \(S\) might fluctuate drastically below or above \(\sigma\), blowing up the tail probabilities.
Resolution: We must assume that the underlying population is normally distributed, \(X_i \overset{\text{i.i.d.}}{\sim} \mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2)\), and determine the exact finite-sample distribution of the ratio: \[ \frac{\bar{X} - \mu}{S/\sqrt{n}} = \frac{\frac{\bar{X} - \mu}{\sigma/\sqrt{n}}}{\sqrt{\frac{S^2}{\sigma^2}}} = \frac{\frac{\bar{X} - \mu}{\sigma/\sqrt{n}}}{\sqrt{\frac{(n-1)S^2/\sigma^2}{n-1}}} = \frac{Z}{\sqrt{W/(n-1)}} \]

6. Theoretical Foundations: The Chi-Squared Distribution \(\chi^2(n)\)

To understand the denominator \(W = \frac{(n-1)S^2}{\sigma^2}\), we study the Chi-Squared distribution.

Definition 6.1 (Chi-Squared Distribution) The Chi-Squared distribution with \(n\) degrees of freedom, written \(X \sim \chi^2(n)\), is defined as a special Gamma distribution: \[ X \sim \text{Gamma}\left(\alpha = \frac{n}{2}, \; \beta = \frac{1}{2}\right) \quad (\text{rate parameterization } \beta = 1/2) \] Its probability density function (PDF) is given by: \[ f_X(x) = \frac{1}{\Gamma(n/2)} \left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{n/2} x^{\frac{n}{2} - 1} e^{-\frac{1}{2}x} \, \mathbb{I}_{(0, \infty)}(x) \] Properties:
  • Mean: \(E[X] = \frac{\alpha}{\beta} = \frac{n/2}{1/2} = n\).
  • Variance: \(\text{Var}(X) = \frac{\alpha}{\beta^2} = \frac{n/2}{(1/2)^2} = 2n\).
  • Moment Generating Function: For \(t < 1/2\): \[ M_X(t) = \left(1 - \frac{t}{1/2}\right)^{-n/2} = \left(\frac{1/2}{1/2 - t}\right)^{n/2} = (1 - 2t)^{-n/2} \]
Theorem 6.2 (The Chi-Squared & Normal Relationship)
  1. Single Squared Normal: If \(Z \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1)\), then \(Y = Z^2 \sim \chi^2(1)\).
    Proof via MGF: \[ M_Y(t) = E[e^{t Z^2}] = \int_{-\infty}^\infty e^{t z^2} \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}} e^{-z^2/2} dz = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}} \int_{-\infty}^\infty e^{-\frac{1}{2}(1 - 2t)z^2} dz = (1 - 2t)^{-1/2} \] which is precisely the MGF of \(\chi^2(1)\).
  2. Additivity of Chi-Squared: Let \(X_1, \dots, X_k\) be independent with \(X_i \sim \chi^2(n_i)\). Then \(Y = \sum_{i=1}^k X_i \sim \chi^2\left(\sum_{i=1}^k n_i\right)\).
    Proof via MGF: \[ M_Y(t) = \prod_{i=1}^k M_{X_i}(t) = \prod_{i=1}^k \left(\frac{1/2}{1/2 - t}\right)^{n_i/2} = \left(\frac{1/2}{1/2 - t}\right)^{\frac{\sum n_i}{2}} \implies Y \sim \chi^2\left(\sum_{i=1}^k n_i\right) \]
  3. Sum of Squared Standard Normals: If \(Z_1, \dots, Z_n \overset{\text{i.i.d.}}{\sim} \mathcal{N}(0, 1)\), then: \[ \sum_{i=1}^n Z_i^2 \sim \chi^2(n) \]

7. Sum-of-Squares Decomposition & MGF Proof of \((n-1)S^2/\sigma^2 \sim \chi^2(n-1)\)

In Slides 25–37 of Lecture 11.3, the complete algebraic and moment-generating proof of the distribution of sample variance is established.

Step 1: Sum-of-Squares Algebraic Identity For an i.i.d. normal sample \(X_1, \dots, X_n \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2)\), add and subtract \(\bar{X}\) inside each squared error term: \[ \sum_{i=1}^n (X_i - \mu)^2 = \sum_{i=1}^n \left[(X_i - \bar{X}) + (\bar{X} - \mu)\right]^2 \] Expanding the quadratic expression: \[ \sum_{i=1}^n (X_i - \mu)^2 = \sum_{i=1}^n (X_i - \bar{X})^2 + 2(\bar{X} - \mu)\sum_{i=1}^n (X_i - \bar{X}) + \sum_{i=1}^n (\bar{X} - \mu)^2 \] Notice that the middle cross-product term vanishes identically: \[ \sum_{i=1}^n (X_i - \bar{X}) = \sum_{i=1}^n X_i - n\bar{X} = n\bar{X} - n\bar{X} = 0 \] Therefore: \[ \sum_{i=1}^n (X_i - \mu)^2 = \sum_{i=1}^n (X_i - \bar{X})^2 + n(\bar{X} - \mu)^2 \]
Step 2: Division by \(\sigma^2\) and MGF Separation Dividing both sides of the identity by \(\sigma^2\): \[ \underbrace{\sum_{i=1}^n \left(\frac{X_i - \mu}{\sigma}\right)^2}_{Y_1} = \underbrace{\frac{\sum_{i=1}^n (X_i - \bar{X})^2}{\sigma^2}}_{Y_2} + \underbrace{\left(\frac{\bar{X} - \mu}{\sigma/\sqrt{n}}\right)^2}_{Y_3} \] Observe each component:
  • LHS (\(Y_1\)): Each \(\frac{X_i - \mu}{\sigma} \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1)\), so \(Y_1\) is the sum of \(n\) independent squared standard normals: \[ Y_1 \sim \chi^2(n) \implies M_{Y_1}(t) = (1 - 2t)^{-n/2} \]
  • RHS second term (\(Y_3\)): \(\frac{\bar{X} - \mu}{\sigma/\sqrt{n}} \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1)\), so its square is: \[ Y_3 \sim \chi^2(1) \implies M_{Y_3}(t) = (1 - 2t)^{-1/2} \]
  • RHS first term (\(Y_2\)): By definition, \(S^2 = \frac{1}{n-1}\sum (X_i - \bar{X})^2\), so: \[ Y_2 = \frac{(n-1)S^2}{\sigma^2} \]
Crucial Independence: In a normal population, the sample mean \(\bar{X}\) and the deviations vector \((X_1 - \bar{X}, \dots, X_n - \bar{X})\) are independent (Fisher's Theorem). Consequently, \(Y_2\) and \(Y_3\) are statistically independent.

Using the MGF property of independent sums: \[ M_{Y_1}(t) = M_{Y_2 + Y_3}(t) = M_{Y_2}(t) \cdot M_{Y_3}(t) \] Solving for \(M_{Y_2}(t)\): \[ M_{Y_2}(t) = \frac{M_{Y_1}(t)}{M_{Y_3}(t)} = \frac{(1 - 2t)^{-n/2}}{(1 - 2t)^{-1/2}} = (1 - 2t)^{-\frac{n-1}{2}} \] By the uniqueness theorem of Moment Generating Functions, \(Y_2\) must follow a Chi-squared distribution: \[ \frac{(n-1)S^2}{\sigma^2} \sim \chi^2(n-1) \] One degree of freedom is lost because estimating the sample mean \(\bar{X}\) imposes the linear constraint \(\sum_{i=1}^n (X_i - \bar{X}) = 0\).

8. Student's t-Distribution: Definition, Jacobian Derivation, & Sensor Lifetime Example

Definition 8.1 (Student's t-Distribution) Let \(Z \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1)\) and \(W \sim \chi^2(n)\) be independent random variables. The ratio: \[ T = \frac{Z}{\sqrt{W / n}} \] is defined as Student's \(t\)-distribution with \(n\) degrees of freedom, written \(T \sim t(n)\).
Bivariate Jacobian Transformation Derivation (Slide 11.3, Slides 15–18) Let \(X_1 = Z \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1)\) and \(X_2 = W \sim \chi^2(n)\) be independent. Their joint density is: \[ f_{X_1, X_2}(x_1, x_2) = \left[\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}} e^{-x_1^2/2}\right] \cdot \left[\frac{1}{\Gamma(n/2) 2^{n/2}} x_2^{n/2 - 1} e^{-x_2/2}\right] \] Define the bivariate transformation: \[ Y_1 = \frac{X_1}{\sqrt{X_2 / n}} = T, \quad Y_2 = X_2 \] The inverse transformations are: \[ x_1 = g_1^{-1}(y_1, y_2) = y_1 \sqrt{\frac{y_2}{n}}, \quad x_2 = g_2^{-1}(y_1, y_2) = y_2 \] The Jacobian matrix of the transformation is: \[ J = \det \begin{pmatrix} \frac{\partial x_1}{\partial y_1} & \frac{\partial x_1}{\partial y_2} \\ \frac{\partial x_2}{\partial y_1} & \frac{\partial x_2}{\partial y_2} \end{pmatrix} = \det \begin{pmatrix} \sqrt{\frac{y_2}{n}} & \frac{y_1}{2\sqrt{n y_2}} \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix} = \sqrt{\frac{y_2}{n}} \] The joint density of \((Y_1, Y_2)\) is \(f_{Y_1, Y_2}(y_1, y_2) = f_{X_1, X_2}(x_1(y_1, y_2), y_2) \cdot |J|\): \[ f_{Y_1, Y_2}(y_1, y_2) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi} \, \Gamma(n/2) 2^{n/2}} \exp\left(-\frac{y_1^2 y_2}{2n}\right) y_2^{\frac{n}{2}-1} e^{-y_2/2} \cdot \left(\frac{y_2}{n}\right)^{1/2} \] \[ f_{Y_1, Y_2}(y_1, y_2) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi n} \, \Gamma(n/2) 2^{n/2}} y_2^{\frac{n+1}{2} - 1} \exp\left(-\frac{y_2}{2}\left[1 + \frac{y_1^2}{n}\right]\right) \] To obtain the marginal density \(f_T(t) = f_{Y_1}(y_1)\), integrate out \(y_2 \in (0, \infty)\): \[ f_T(t) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi n} \, \Gamma(n/2) 2^{n/2}} \int_0^\infty y_2^{\frac{n+1}{2} - 1} \exp\left(- y_2 \cdot \frac{1 + t^2/n}{2}\right) dy_2 \] Recognizing the integral as the kernel of a \(\text{Gamma}\left(\frac{n+1}{2}, \frac{1 + t^2/n}{2}\right)\) density, its value is: \[ \frac{\Gamma\left(\frac{n+1}{2}\right)}{\left(\frac{1 + t^2/n}{2}\right)^{\frac{n+1}{2}}} \] Substituting this back: \[ f_T(t) = \frac{\Gamma\left(\frac{n+1}{2}\right)}{\Gamma(n/2)\sqrt{n\pi}} \left(1 + \frac{t^2}{n}\right)^{-\frac{n+1}{2}}, \quad -\infty < t < \infty \] This establishes the exact density of Student's \(t\)-distribution with \(n\) degrees of freedom!
Moments and Asymptotic Behavior of \(t(n)\) (Slide 11.3, Slides 20–22)
  • Mean: \(E[T] = 0\) for \(n > 1\) (undefined for \(n = 1\), the Cauchy distribution).
  • Variance: \[ \text{Var}(T) = \frac{n}{n - 2} \quad \text{for } n > 2 \] Notice that \(\text{Var}(T) > 1\) for all finite \(n\), reflecting the heavier tails caused by uncertainty in estimating \(\sigma\).
  • Asymptotic Normality: As degrees of freedom \(n \to \infty\): \[ \lim_{n \to \infty} \left(1 + \frac{t^2}{n}\right)^{-\frac{n+1}{2}} = e^{-t^2/2} \implies t(n) \xrightarrow{d} \mathcal{N}(0, 1) \]
Worked Slide Example 11.2: Continuous Glucose Monitoring Sensor Lifetimes Problem Statement: A biomedical engineering study tests a new sensor for a continuous glucose monitoring (CGM) system. Previous clinical experience indicates that the lifetime of the sensors (in days) is normally distributed.

A random sample of \(n = 20\) patients is fitted with the new sensor. On average, the sensors operated for \(\bar{x} = 187\) days before wearing out. The sample variance of sensor lifetimes was measured at \(s^2 = 16.2\) \(\text{days}^2\) (hence \(s = \sqrt{16.2} \approx 4.025\) days).

Find a \(95\%\) confidence interval for the true mean sensor lifetime \(\mu\).

Step-by-Step Solution:
  1. Identify the setting: Normal population, \(\sigma^2\) unknown, small sample size \(n = 20 \le 30\). We must use Student's \(t\)-distribution with \(\text{df} = n - 1 = 19\).
  2. Identify critical value \(t_{\alpha/2, n-1}\): Here \(1 - \alpha = 0.95 \implies \alpha = 0.05 \implies \alpha/2 = 0.025\). We need \(t_{0.025, 19}\). In R: 
    > qt(0.975, df = 19)
[1] 2.093024  # rounded to 2.093
  3. Confidence Interval Formula: \[ \bar{x} \pm t_{\alpha/2, n-1} \frac{s}{\sqrt{n}} = 187 \pm 2.093 \frac{\sqrt{16.2}}{\sqrt{20}} \]
  4. Calculate Margin of Error: \[ \frac{\sqrt{16.2}}{\sqrt{20}} = \sqrt{\frac{16.2}{20}} = \sqrt{0.81} = 0.9000 \] \[ E = 2.093024 \times 0.9000 \approx 1.8837 \approx 1.88 \text{ days} \]
  5. Compute bounds: \[ [187 - 1.88, \; 187 + 1.88] \implies (185.11, \; 188.88) \]
Conclusion: We are \(95\%\) confident that the true average lifetime of the new CGM sensors across all diabetic patients is between \(185.11\) and \(188.88\) days.

9. Comprehensive Summary of Univariate Mean Inference Methods

The following decision framework synthesizes all three slide sets for constructing confidence intervals for a population mean \(\mu\):

Inference Selection Matrix for Population Mean \(\mu\)
Population Dist. Variance \(\sigma^2\) Sample Size \(n\) Pivotal Statistic \(100(1-\alpha)\%\) Confidence Interval
Normal Known Any \(n\) \(Z = \frac{\bar{X}-\mu}{\sigma/\sqrt{n}} \sim \mathcal{N}(0,1)\) \(\bar{X} \pm z_{\alpha/2}\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}\) (Exact)
Any with finite \(\sigma^2\) Known Large (\(n > 30\)) \(Z \approx \frac{\bar{X}-\mu}{\sigma/\sqrt{n}} \xrightarrow{d} \mathcal{N}(0,1)\) \(\bar{X} \pm z_{\alpha/2}\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}\) (CLT Approximate)
Any with finite \(\sigma^2\) Unknown Large (\(n > 30\)) \(\frac{\bar{X}-\mu}{S/\sqrt{n}} \xrightarrow{d} \mathcal{N}(0,1)\) \(\bar{X} \pm z_{\alpha/2}\frac{S}{\sqrt{n}}\) (Slutsky Approximate)
Normal Unknown Small (\(n \le 30\)) \(T = \frac{\bar{X}-\mu}{S/\sqrt{n}} \sim t(n-1)\) \(\bar{X} \pm t_{\alpha/2, n-1}\frac{S}{\sqrt{n}}\) (Exact)
Non-Normal Unknown Small (\(n \le 30\)) Distribution non-standard No parametric CI! (Use Bootstrap / Nonparametric)
