1. Non-Linear Feature Mappings (\(\phi(x)\)) & Dimensionality Explosion

In linear regression and linear classification, our hypothesis assumes linearity directly over the input coordinates \(x \in \mathbb{R}^d\): \[ h_\theta(x) = \theta_0 + \theta_1 x_1 + \dots + \theta_d x_d = \theta^T x \] However, real-world data science problems routinely present complex non-linear manifolds that no flat hyperplane can separate or fit. The classical paradigm to overcome this limitation is to map the raw input vector \(x \in \mathbb{R}^d\) into a high-dimensional (or even infinite-dimensional) feature space \(\mathcal{H}\) via a non-linear vector-valued map: \[ \phi: \mathbb{R}^d \to \mathbb{R}^p \quad (p \gg d) \] Once mapped, we fit a linear model over \(\phi(x)\): \[ h_\theta(x) = \theta^T \phi(x) = \sum_{j=1}^p \theta_j \phi_j(x) \]

Example: 1D Input to Cubic Polynomial Feature Space Consider a 1-dimensional input feature \(x \in \mathbb{R}\). We define the cubic polynomial feature map: \[ \phi(x) = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ x \\ x^2 \\ x^3 \end{bmatrix} \in \mathbb{R}^4 \] The hypothesis evaluated in this feature space becomes: \[ h_\theta(x) = \theta^T \phi(x) = \theta_0 + \theta_1 x + \theta_2 x^2 + \theta_3 x^3 \] While \(h_\theta(x)\) produces a highly expressive, non-linear cubic curve in the original attribute space \(\mathbb{R}\), it remains strictly linear with respect to the parameter vector \(\theta \in \mathbb{R}^4\)!
The Curse of Dimensionality in Explicit Feature Mappings

When \(x \in \mathbb{R}^d\) contains multiple features, expanding all monomial interactions up to degree \(k\) causes a catastrophic combinatorial explosion in dimensionality. For degree \(k \le 3\): \[ p = \binom{d + k}{k} \approx O(d^3) \] For an image patch with \(d = 1000\) pixels, computing degree-3 polynomial features yields \(p \approx \frac{1000^3}{6} \approx 1.6 \times 10^8\) dimensions. Storing, computing, and optimizing \(\theta \in \mathbb{R}^{1.6 \times 10^8}\) is intractable. This computational barrier motivates the Dual Representation and the Kernel Trick.

2. Dual Representation of Linear Models & Dual LMS Updates

Let us examine Least Mean Squares (LMS) gradient descent on a dataset of \(m\) training examples \(\{(x^{(i)}, y^{(i)})\}_{i=1}^m\) where features are mapped via \(\phi(x) \in \mathbb{R}^p\). The batch cost function is: \[ J(\theta) = \frac{1}{2} \sum_{i=1}^m \left(y^{(i)} - \theta^T \phi(x^{(i)})\right)^2 \] The gradient descent parameter update rule is: \[ \theta := \theta + \alpha \sum_{i=1}^m \left(y^{(i)} - \theta^T \phi(x^{(i)})\right) \phi(x^{(i)}) \]

The Dual Representation Theorem for LMS Suppose the parameter vector is initialized to zero: \(\theta^{(0)} = \mathbf{0}\). Then after any number of iterations, \(\theta\) must lie entirely within the linear span of the transformed training inputs: \[ \theta = \sum_{i=1}^m \beta_i \phi(x^{(i)}) \] where \(\beta = [\beta_1, \beta_2, \dots, \beta_m]^T \in \mathbb{R}^m\) is a vector of \(m\) dual coefficients (one coefficient per training sample).
Derivation: Inductive Step for Dual LMS Substitute the dual expansion \(\theta = \sum_{j=1}^m \beta_j \phi(x^{(j)})\) into the prediction for sample \(i\): \[ \theta^T \phi(x^{(i)}) = \left( \sum_{j=1}^m \beta_j \phi(x^{(j)}) \right)^T \phi(x^{(i)}) = \sum_{j=1}^m \beta_j \langle \phi(x^{(j)}), \phi(x^{(i)}) \rangle \] Define the Gram Matrix (Kernel Matrix) \(K \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times m}\) such that: \[ K_{ij} = \langle \phi(x^{(i)}), \phi(x^{(j)}) \rangle = \phi(x^{(i)})^T \phi(x^{(j)}) \] Then the model's prediction on the \(i\)-th training instance is the \(i\)-th row of the matrix-vector product \(K \beta\): \[ \theta^T \phi(x^{(i)}) = (K \beta)_i \] Now, looking at the batch LMS update rule: \[ \theta^{(\text{new})} = \theta^{(\text{old})} + \alpha \sum_{i=1}^m \left(y^{(i)} - (K \beta)_i \right) \phi(x^{(i)}) \] Equating this to \(\sum_{i=1}^m \beta_i^{(\text{new})} \phi(x^{(i)})\), we obtain the exact vectorized update rule for the dual parameters \(\beta \in \mathbb{R}^m\): \[ \beta := \beta + \alpha \left(\vec{y} - K \beta\right) \] where \(\vec{y} = [y^{(1)}, \dots, y^{(m)}]^T \in \mathbb{R}^m\).

To compute the hypothesis on an unseen query point \(x \in \mathbb{R}^d\), we never need \(\theta\) explicitly: \[ h_\theta(x) = \theta^T \phi(x) = \left( \sum_{i=1}^m \beta_i \phi(x^{(i)}) \right)^T \phi(x) = \sum_{i=1}^m \beta_i \langle \phi(x^{(i)}), \phi(x) \rangle \]

Dimensionality Comparison: Primal vs. Dual Formulations

In the primal formulation, the parameter vector \(\theta \in \mathbb{R}^p\) scales with the feature dimension, which may be millions of dimensions or even infinite. In the dual representation, we optimize the dual vector \(\beta \in \mathbb{R}^m\), whose dimension depends strictly on the number of training examples (\(m\)): \[ \text{Primal parameter: } \theta \in \mathbb{R}^p \quad \longleftrightarrow \quad \text{Dual parameter: } \beta \in \mathbb{R}^m \] Furthermore, both model training and evaluation depend strictly on inner products between input pairs:

3. The Kernel Trick & Inner Product Computation

The realization that both the optimization of \(\beta\) and the evaluation of \(h_\theta(x)\) depend exclusively on pairwise inner products \(\langle \phi(x), \phi(z) \rangle\) gives birth to the Kernel Trick.

Definition 3.1: The Kernel Function Given an implicit or explicit feature map \(\phi: \mathcal{X} \to \mathcal{H}\), the corresponding Kernel function \(K: \mathcal{X} \times \mathcal{X} \to \mathbb{R}\) evaluates the inner product between transformed vectors in \(\mathcal{H}\): \[ K(x, z) \triangleq \langle \phi(x), \phi(z) \rangle = \phi(x)^T \phi(z) \]

The Core Insight: In many cases, \(K(x, z)\) can be evaluated in closed form in \(O(d)\) arithmetic operations in the original input space, completely bypassing the construction, storage, and computation of the \(p\)-dimensional vector \(\phi(x)\).

4. Canonical Kernels: Polynomial & Gaussian RBF

4.1 The Homogeneous & Inhomogeneous Polynomial Kernels

Consider the quadratic polynomial kernel for \(x, z \in \mathbb{R}^2\): \[ K(x, z) = (x^T z)^2 \] Expanding algebraically: \[ K(x, z) = (x_1 z_1 + x_2 z_2)^2 = x_1^2 z_1^2 + 2 x_1 x_2 z_1 z_2 + x_2^2 z_2^2 \] This equals the inner product \(\phi(x)^T \phi(z)\) where: \[ \phi(x) = \begin{bmatrix} x_1^2 \\ \sqrt{2} x_1 x_2 \\ x_2^2 \end{bmatrix}, \quad \phi(z) = \begin{bmatrix} z_1^2 \\ \sqrt{2} z_1 z_2 \\ z_2^2 \end{bmatrix} \] Evaluating \(\phi(x)\) and \(\phi(z)\) explicitly and computing their dot product requires \(O(d^2)\) time. In contrast, calculating \((x^T z)^2\) takes only \(O(d)\) operations (one dot product plus one scalar squaring)!

General Polynomial Kernel For arbitrary dimension \(d\), degree \(k \ge 1\), and intercept \(c \ge 0\): \[ K(x, z) = (x^T z + c)^k \] For \(k=2\), expanding \((x^T z + c)^2 = (x^T z)^2 + 2c(x^T z) + c^2\) captures:
  • All quadratic interaction terms: \(x_i x_j\) with scaling \(\sqrt{2}\) for \(i \ne j\) and \(1\) for \(i=j\).
  • All linear terms: \(\sqrt{2c}\, x_i\).
  • A constant bias term: \(c\).
The resulting feature space dimensionality is \(p = \binom{d+2}{2} = \frac{(d+2)(d+1)}{2}\), yet evaluating the kernel takes merely \(O(d)\) time!

4.2 The Gaussian Radial Basis Function (RBF) Kernel

The most widely used non-linear kernel in machine learning is the Gaussian RBF kernel: \[ K(x, z) = \exp\left( -\frac{\|x - z\|^2}{2\sigma^2} \right) = \exp\left( -\gamma \|x - z\|^2 \right) \] where \(\sigma > 0\) is the Gaussian bandwidth (standard deviation) and \(\gamma = \frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\) controls the decay rate.

5. Infinite-Dimensional Feature Space of Gaussian RBF

What explicit feature map \(\phi(x)\) corresponds to the Gaussian RBF kernel? We prove that \(\phi(x)\) resides in an infinite-dimensional Hilbert space \(\ell_2\).

Mathematical Proof: Infinite-Dimensional Expansion Consider scalar inputs \(x, z \in \mathbb{R}\) with bandwidth \(\sigma = 1\). Expand the squared Euclidean difference: \[ \|x - z\|^2 = x^2 - 2xz + z^2 \] Substituting into the kernel definition: \[ K(x, z) = \exp\left(-\frac{x^2 - 2xz + z^2}{2}\right) = \exp\left(-\frac{x^2}{2}\right) \exp\left(-\frac{z^2}{2}\right) \exp(xz) \] Recall the Taylor series expansion of the exponential function: \(\exp(u) = \sum_{k=0}^\infty \frac{u^k}{k!}\). Applying this to \(\exp(xz)\): \[ K(x, z) = e^{-x^2/2} e^{-z^2/2} \sum_{k=0}^\infty \frac{(xz)^k}{k!} = \sum_{k=0}^\infty \left( e^{-x^2/2} \frac{x^k}{\sqrt{k!}} \right) \left( e^{-z^2/2} \frac{z^k}{\sqrt{k!}} \right) \] Rewriting this infinite sum as a vector inner product: \[ K(x, z) = \phi(x)^T \phi(z) \] where the explicit feature map \(\phi(x)\) is the infinite-dimensional vector: \[ \phi(x) = e^{-x^2/2} \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ x \\ \frac{x^2}{\sqrt{2!}} \\ \frac{x^3}{\sqrt{3!}} \\ \vdots \\ \frac{x^k}{\sqrt{k!}} \\ \vdots \end{bmatrix} \in \mathbb{R}^\infty \] Profound Conclusion: Computing with the Gaussian RBF kernel allows us to perform exact linear regression or classification in an infinite-dimensional feature space containing polynomial terms of every conceivable order \(k \in \{0, 1, 2, \dots, \infty\}\), while executing in mere \(O(d)\) time!

6. Mercer's Theorem & Positive Semi-Definite Gram Matrices

Given a candidate mathematical function \(K(x, z)\), how can we guarantee that there exists a valid Hilbert feature space \(\mathcal{H}\) and mapping \(\phi(x)\) such that \(K(x, z) = \langle \phi(x), \phi(z) \rangle\)?

Definition 6.1: The Gram Matrix Given an arbitrary collection of \(m\) data points \(\{x^{(1)}, \dots, x^{(m)}\}\), the Gram Matrix (or Kernel Matrix) \(K \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times m}\) is defined element-wise by: \[ K_{ij} = K(x^{(i)}, x^{(j)}) \]
Theorem 6.1: Mercer's Condition A symmetric function \(K: \mathcal{X} \times \mathcal{X} \to \mathbb{R}\) (where \(K(x, z) = K(z, x)\)) is a valid Mercer Kernel if and only if for any finite set of points \(\{x^{(1)}, \dots, x^{(m)}\}\), the resulting Gram matrix \(K\) is Positive Semi-Definite (PSD): \[ z^T K z \ge 0 \quad \forall z \in \mathbb{R}^m \]
Proof: Necessity of Positive Semi-Definiteness If \(K\) is a valid kernel, then by definition \(K(x^{(i)}, x^{(j)}) = \phi(x^{(i)})^T \phi(x^{(j)}) = \sum_k \phi_k(x^{(i)}) \phi_k(x^{(j)})\). Let \(z \in \mathbb{R}^m\) be an arbitrary non-zero vector. Then: \[ \begin{aligned} z^T K z &= \sum_{i=1}^m \sum_{j=1}^m z_i z_j K_{ij} \\ &= \sum_{i=1}^m \sum_{j=1}^m z_i z_j \left( \sum_k \phi_k(x^{(i)}) \phi_k(x^{(j)}) \right) \\ &= \sum_k \left( \sum_{i=1}^m z_i \phi_k(x^{(i)}) \right) \left( \sum_{j=1}^m z_j \phi_k(x^{(j)}) \right) \\ &= \sum_k \left( \sum_{i=1}^m z_i \phi_k(x^{(i)}) \right)^2 \ge 0 \end{aligned} \] Because the sum of squared real terms is always non-negative, \(z^T K z \ge 0\) is always satisfied!

7. Similarity Metric Perspective & Landmark Representation

Beyond linear algebra and dot products, kernels can be interpreted intuitively as generalized similarity metrics:

  • Identity: If \(x = z\), then \(\|x - z\| = 0 \implies K(x, x) = \exp(0) = 1\) (maximum similarity).
  • Orthogonality / Divergence: As \(\|x - z\| \to \infty\), \(K(x, z) \to 0\) (zero similarity).
The Landmark Formulation Suppose we manually select reference points in feature space called landmarks \(l^{(1)}, l^{(2)}, \dots \in \mathbb{R}^d\). For any sample \(x\), we construct a new feature vector based on its similarity to each landmark: \[ f_1 = K(x, l^{(1)}) = \exp\left(-\frac{\|x - l^{(1)}\|^2}{2\sigma^2}\right), \quad f_2 = K(x, l^{(2)}) = \exp\left(-\frac{\|x - l^{(2)}\|^2}{2\sigma^2}\right), \quad \dots \]

Role of Bandwidth \(\sigma^2\):

  • Small \(\sigma^2\) (e.g. \(\sigma^2 = 0.5\)): The Gaussian falls off extremely steeply. Only points immediately adjacent to landmark \(l^{(1)}\) have \(f_1 \approx 1\). This yields high variance, sharp non-linear boundaries, and risk of overfitting.
  • Large \(\sigma^2\) (e.g. \(\sigma^2 = 3\)): The Gaussian bell is wide and flat. Even distant points retain moderate similarity \(f_1 > 0\). This yields high bias, smoother boundaries, and higher regularization.

8. Hyperplanes, Margins, and Confidence in Classification

We now turn our attention from kernelized regression to classification, establishing the mathematical foundations of Support Vector Machines.

8.1 Geometry of a Separating Hyperplane

In a \(d\)-dimensional input space, a hyperplane is defined algebraically by: \[ w_0 + w_1 x_1 + w_2 x_2 + \dots + w_d x_d = 0 \] In vector notation, partitioning the intercept (bias) \(b = w_0 = \theta_0\) and weight vector \(w = [w_1, \dots, w_d]^T \in \mathbb{R}^d\): \[ w^T x + b = 0 \] The vector \(w\) is orthogonal (perpendicular) to the hyperplane, and \(b / \|w\|\) determines the signed distance from the origin.

8.2 Margins & Prediction Confidence

In binary classification, let targets be labeled \(y \in \{-1, +1\}\). A linear classifier makes predictions via the sign of the linear combination: \[ h_{w, b}(x) = \text{sign}(w^T x + b) \] A sample \(x^{(i)}\) is correctly classified if and only if: \[ y^{(i)} (w^T x^{(i)} + b) > 0 \]

Slide 30 Inspection: Connection to Logistic Regression Confidence

Recall in logistic regression, \(h_\theta(x) = \sigma(\theta^T x) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-\theta^T x}}\): \[ \begin{aligned} \sigma(\theta^T x) > 0.5 &\iff \theta^T x > 0 \implies \hat{y} = 1 \\ \sigma(\theta^T x) < 0.5 &\iff \theta^T x < 0 \implies \hat{y} = 0 \text{ (or } -1 \text{)} \end{aligned} \] To classify with high confidence, we desire: \[ \begin{aligned} \text{If } y = +1 &\implies w^T x + b \gg 0 \\ \text{If } y = -1 &\implies w^T x + b \ll 0 \end{aligned} \] Examining three points \(A, B, C\) relative to the boundary:

  • Point A (Far away): \(|w^T x_A + b| \gg 0\). We are extremely confident in our prediction. Perturbing \(x_A\) slightly will never change its classification.
  • Point B (Intermediate): Confident, but closer to the decision boundary.
  • Point C (Very close): \(|w^T x_C + b| \approx 0\). Very low confidence. Even tiny noise in data collection or slight rotation of the boundary flips the predicted class!

9. The Maximal Margin Classifier (MMC) & Support Vectors

If training data is linearly separable, infinitely many separating hyperplanes satisfy \(y^{(i)}(w^T x^{(i)} + b) > 0\) for all \(i = 1 \dots m\). Which one is optimal?

Definition 9.1: The Geometric Margin The perpendicular distance from any point \(x^{(i)}\) to the hyperplane \(w^T x + b = 0\) is: \[ \gamma^{(i)} = \frac{y^{(i)} (w^T x^{(i)} + b)}{\|w\|_2} \] The margin of the dataset, denoted \(M\), is the minimum perpendicular distance from any training point to the separating hyperplane: \[ M = \min_{i=1 \dots m} \frac{y^{(i)} (w^T x^{(i)} + b)}{\|w\|_2} \]

Geometric Margin: The margin represents the minimum perpendicular Euclidean distance from any sample point \(x^{(i)}\) to the decision hyperplane.

Optimization Formulation: Maximal Margin Classifier (MMC) We seek the hyperplane that maximizes this minimum distance \(M\): \[ \begin{aligned} \max_{w_0, w_1, \dots, w_n, M} \quad & M \\ \text{subject to} \quad & \sum_{j=1}^n w_j^2 = 1 \quad (\|w\|_2 = 1) \\ & y^{(i)} (w_0 + w_1 x_1^{(i)} + \dots + w_n x_n^{(i)}) \ge M, \quad \forall i = 1 \dots m \end{aligned} \]

Why enforce \(\sum_{j=1}^n w_j^2 = 1\)?
Enforcing the scale normalization \(\|w\|_2 = 1\) guarantees that the functional margin corresponds to the true Euclidean perpendicular distance. Without fixing the scale of \(w\), multiplying all weights by an arbitrary scalar \(k > 0\) leaves the physical hyperplane \(k(w^T x + b) = 0\) unchanged while scaling the algebraic product arbitrarily.

Definition 9.2: Support Vectors (SV) The points that achieve the equality \(y^{(i)}(w^T x^{(i)} + b) = M\) lie directly on the margin boundaries. These critical points are named Support Vectors.

If any training point not on the margin boundary is moved slightly (or deleted entirely), the optimal Maximal Margin Hyperplane (MMH) does not move at all. The solution is completely determined and "supported" by the support vectors alone!

10. Support Vector Classifiers (SVC) & Slack Budget (\(C\))

The Maximal Margin Classifier suffers from two severe practical flaws:

  1. Linear Inseparability: If the data cannot be perfectly partitioned by a line/plane, no feasible solution exists (\(M\) cannot be positive).
  2. Extreme Sensitivity to Outliers: Even if separable, a single outlier can dramatically tilt the hyperplane (as illustrated on slide 27, shifting the separating line from a balanced blue orientation to an overfitted, unstable red orientation).
The Soft Margin Support Vector Classifier (Cortes & Vapnik) To build a robust classifier that tolerates noise and yields better generalization, we introduce non-negative slack variables \(\epsilon_1, \epsilon_2, \dots, \epsilon_m \ge 0\): \[ \begin{aligned} \max_{w, b, \epsilon, M} \quad & M \\ \text{subject to} \quad & \sum_{j=1}^n w_j^2 = 1 \\ & y^{(i)} (w^T x^{(i)} + b) \ge M(1 - \epsilon_i), \quad \forall i = 1 \dots m \\ & \epsilon_i \ge 0, \quad \sum_{i=1}^m \epsilon_i \le C \end{aligned} \] where \(C > 0\) is a user-defined slack budget parameter.

10.1 Complete Taxonomy of the Slack Variable \(\epsilon_i\)

Geometric interpretation of slack variables \(\xi_i\):

Value of Slack \(\epsilon_i\) Geometric Location Status of Observation \(x^{(i)}\)
\(\epsilon_i = 0\) Correctly classified, on or outside margin boundary Correctly classified, outside or strictly on the margin boundary. Satisfies \(y^{(i)}(w^T x^{(i)} + b) \ge M\).
\(\epsilon_i > 0\) Margin violation (inside margin boundary) Margin violation: Point has penetrated the margin buffer (\(y^{(i)}(w^T x^{(i)} + b) < M\)).
\(0 < \epsilon_i \le 1\) Correctly classified but within margin band (\(0 < \xi_i < 1\)) Inside margin, but on the correct side of the hyperplane. Still correctly classified!
\(\epsilon_i > 1\) Misclassified on wrong side of hyperplane (\(\xi_i > 1\)) Misclassified: Point has crossed the decision boundary onto the incorrect side.

10.2 The Role of the Budget Parameter \(C\)

The Bias-Variance Trade-off Governed by \(C\)

The parameter \(C = \sum_{i=1}^m \epsilon_i\) limits the total distance by which observations can violate the margin:

  • When \(C = 0\): No slack is permitted (\(\epsilon_i = 0 \; \forall i\)). This collapses back to the rigid Maximal Margin Classifier (MMC). Only possible if data is linearly separable.
  • When \(C\) is Large (\(C \uparrow\)):
    • We tolerate wider and more frequent margin violations.
    • The margin \(M\) becomes wider (\(M \uparrow\)).
    • More data points lie inside the margin or violate it \(\implies\) Number of Support Vectors increases.
    • The decision boundary becomes less sensitive to individual data points \(\implies\) Bias increases (\(\text{bias} \uparrow\)), Variance decreases (\(\text{var} \downarrow\)).
  • When \(C\) is Small (\(C \downarrow\)):
    • Violations are strictly penalized.
    • The margin \(M\) becomes narrower (\(M \downarrow\)).
    • Fewer points violate the margin \(\implies\) Number of Support Vectors decreases.
    • The hyperplane is tightly fitted to individual boundary points \(\implies\) Bias decreases (\(\text{bias} \downarrow\)), Variance increases (\(\text{var} \uparrow\)) (risk of overfitting).

11. The Grand Taxonomy: MMC \(\to\) SVC \(\to\) SVM

Slide 29 outlines the elegant conceptual hierarchy that unifies maximum-margin learning:

Three Stages of Support Vector Architecture
  1. 1. Maximal Margin Classifier (MMC):
    Hard-margin linear classifier. Assumes strict linear separability; maximizes the perpendicular geometric margin \(M\). Highly sensitive to individual boundary points.
  2. 2. Support Vector Classifier (SVC):
    Soft-margin linear classifier. Introduces slack variables \(\epsilon_i \ge 0\) and budget \(C\). Tolerates non-separable noise and robustly classifies non-separable datasets.
  3. 3. Support Vector Machine (SVM):
    Support Vector Classifier + Kernel Trick (\(x \longrightarrow \phi(x)\)).
    By substituting inner products \(\langle x^{(i)}, x^{(j)} \rangle\) with a non-linear Mercer kernel \(K(x^{(i)}, x^{(j)})\), the soft-margin linear classifier finds non-linear, complex decision boundaries in high-dimensional or infinite-dimensional Hilbert spaces with optimal computational efficiency!
