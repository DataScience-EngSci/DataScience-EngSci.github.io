1. Foundations Review: Non-Linear Hypotheses & Multi-Class Vector Targets

In Lecture 20, we established that classical linear models (such as Linear Regression and Logistic Regression) fail catastrophically when applied to high-dimensional, complex perceptual tasks such as computer vision. When considering non-linear polynomial expansions (e.g., all quadratic combinations \(x_i x_j\)), a modest \(50 \times 50\) grayscale image yields \(\approx \frac{2500^2}{2} \approx 3 \times 10^6\) features, and an RGB image yields upwards of \(28 \times 10^6\) features. This explosive parameter complexity leads to severe overfitting and intractable computation.

Artificial Neural Networks (ANNs) resolve this challenge by learning hierarchical representations \(\phi_\beta(x)\) through successive layers of non-linear transformations.

Definition 1.1 (Multi-Layer Perceptron Notation & Indexing) Let a feedforward neural network have \(n_l\) total layers, where layer \(1\) denotes the input layer, layers \(2, \dots, n_l - 1\) are hidden layers, and layer \(n_l\) is the output layer.
  • Let \(s_l\) denote the number of physical activation units in layer \(l\) (excluding the bias unit \(a_0^{(l)} = +1\)).
  • The input vector is \(x \in \mathbb{R}^{s_1}\). We set the initial activations \(a^{(1)} = x\).
  • For each layer \(l \in \{1, \dots, n_l - 1\}\), the weight parameter matrix connecting layer \(l\) to layer \(l+1\) is denoted \(\Theta^{(l)} \in \mathbb{R}^{s_{l+1} \times (s_l + 1)}\) (or alternatively \(W^{(l)} \in \mathbb{R}^{s_{l+1} \times s_l}\) with separate bias vector \(b^{(l)} \in \mathbb{R}^{s_{l+1}}\)).
  • Entry \(\Theta_{ji}^{(l)}\) (or \(W_{ji}^{(l)}\)) denotes the weight of the synapse leading from unit \(i\) in layer \(l\) into unit \(j\) in layer \(l+1\): \[ \text{Dimension of } \Theta^{(l)} = (\text{units in layer } l+1) \times (\text{units in layer } l + 1) \]

For multi-class classification with \(K\) distinct categories (e.g., pedestrian, car, motorcycle, truck), the target is represented as a one-hot column vector \(y \in \mathbb{R}^K\):

\[ y \in \left\{ \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}, \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}, \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}, \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \\ 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} \right\} \subset \mathbb{R}^4 \]

The output layer has \(s_{n_l} = K\) units, yielding an estimated probability distribution \(h_\Theta(x) = a^{(n_l)} \in \mathbb{R}^K\) where each component \((h_\Theta(x))_k \approx P(y_k = 1 \mid x)\).

2. Non-Linear Decision Boundaries via Logic Synthesis: AND, OR, NOT & XNOR

Historically, the Perceptron was famously criticized by Minsky and Papert (1969) because a single linear threshold unit cannot compute the non-linear XOR (Exclusive OR) or XNOR (Equivalence) function. However, as demonstrated in the lecture slides, cascading elementary logistic units into a multi-layer network synthesizes arbitrary Boolean logic functions with razor-sharp non-linear decision boundaries.

The Sigmoid Thresholding Regime Recall the logistic sigmoid activation function \(g(z) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-z}}\). For large positive inputs \(z \ge 4.6\), \(g(z) \approx 1\); for large negative inputs \(z \le -4.6\), \(g(z) \approx 0\); and \(g(0) = 0.5\). By selecting weights of magnitude \(\approx 20\) to \(40\), a continuous sigmoid unit mimics a discrete Boolean gate.

Building Elementary Boolean Gates

Let inputs \(x_1, x_2 \in \{0, 1\}\). We incorporate the constant bias \(x_0 = +1\).

Example 2.1 (Logical AND Gate) Consider parameters \(\Theta^{(1)} = \begin{bmatrix} -30 & 20 & 20 \end{bmatrix}\). The hypothesis computes: \[ h_\Theta(x) = g\left(-30 + 20x_1 + 20x_2\right) \] Evaluating across all Boolean input combinations:
\(x_1\) \(x_2\) Pre-activation \(z = -30 + 20x_1 + 20x_2\) \(h_\Theta(x) = g(z)\) Boolean Target \(x_1 \land x_2\)
0 0 \(-30\) \(g(-30) \approx 0\) 0
0 1 \(-10\) \(g(-10) \approx 0\) 0
1 0 \(-10\) \(g(-10) \approx 0\) 0
1 1 \(+10\) \(g(+10) \approx 1\) 1
This single unit reproduces the exact truth table of logical AND.
Example 2.2 (Logical OR, NOT & NOR Gates)
  • Logical OR Gate: Choose \(\Theta^{(1)} = \begin{bmatrix} -10 & 20 & 20 \end{bmatrix}\). \[ h_\Theta(x) = g(-10 + 20x_1 + 20x_2) \] For \((0, 0)\), \(z = -10 \implies g(-10) \approx 0\). For \((0, 1)\) or \((1, 0)\), \(z = 10 \implies g(10) \approx 1\). For \((1, 1)\), \(z = 30 \implies g(30) \approx 1\).
  • Logical NOT Gate: For a single scalar input \(x_1 \in \{0, 1\}\), choose \(\Theta^{(1)} = \begin{bmatrix} 10 & -20 \end{bmatrix}\). \[ h_\Theta(x) = g(10 - 20x_1) \implies \begin{cases} x_1 = 0 \implies g(10) \approx 1 \\ x_1 = 1 \implies g(-10) \approx 0 \end{cases} \]
  • Logical NOR Gate (NOT \(x_1\) AND NOT \(x_2\)): Choose \(\Theta^{(1)} = \begin{bmatrix} 10 & -20 & -20 \end{bmatrix}\). \[ h_\Theta(x) = g(10 - 20x_1 - 20x_2) \] Only \((0,0)\) yields \(z = 10 \implies 1\); all other input pairs yield \(z \le -10 \implies 0\).

Synthesizing Non-Linear XNOR via a 2-Layer Network

The XNOR function evaluates to \(1\) if and only if \(x_1\) and \(x_2\) are identical (\(x_1 = x_2\)). It is expressed in terms of disjunctive normal form as: \[ x_1 \text{ XNOR } x_2 = (x_1 \land x_2) \lor (\neg x_1 \land \neg x_2) = (x_1 \text{ AND } x_2) \text{ OR } (x_1 \text{ NOR } x_2) \]

Theorem 2.1 (Two-Layer Synthesis of XNOR) We construct a neural network with \(s_1 = 2\) inputs, a hidden layer of \(s_2 = 2\) neurons, and \(s_3 = 1\) output neuron.

Hidden Layer 2: \[ \Theta^{(1)} = \begin{bmatrix} -30 & 20 & 20 \\ 10 & -20 & -20 \end{bmatrix} \in \mathbb{R}^{2 \times 3} \] The first hidden neuron computes \(a_1^{(2)} = g(-30 + 20x_1 + 20x_2) \approx x_1 \text{ AND } x_2\).
The second hidden neuron computes \(a_2^{(2)} = g(10 - 20x_1 - 20x_2) \approx (\text{NOT } x_1) \text{ AND } (\text{NOT } x_2)\).

Output Layer 3 (OR Gate over Hidden Activations): We append bias \(a_0^{(2)} = 1\), so \(a^{(2)} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & a_1^{(2)} & a_2^{(2)} \end{bmatrix}^T\). Setting \(\Theta^{(2)} = \begin{bmatrix} -10 & 20 & 20 \end{bmatrix} \in \mathbb{R}^{1 \times 3}\): \[ a_1^{(3)} = g\left(-10 + 20 a_1^{(2)} + 20 a_2^{(2)}\right) \]
\(x_1\) \(x_2\) \(a_1^{(2)} \approx x_1 \text{ AND } x_2\) \(a_2^{(2)} \approx \text{NOR}(x_1, x_2)\) \(a_1^{(3)} = g(-10 + 20a_1^{(2)} + 20a_2^{(2)})\) XNOR Output
0 0 0 1 \(g(10) \approx 1\) 1
0 1 0 0 \(g(-10) \approx 0\) 0
1 0 0 0 \(g(-10) \approx 0\) 0
1 1 1 0 \(g(10) \approx 1\) 1
By combining linear separators in the hidden layer, the network synthesizes an enclosed, non-linear decision boundary separating the diagonal XOR/XNOR classes!

3. Objective Functions: Single-Sample Loss, Regularized MSE & Multi-Class Cross-Entropy

To train the parameters \(\Theta\) (or \(\{W, b\}\)) via gradient-based optimization, we define an objective function measuring the discrepancy between predictions and ground-truth targets across a training set \(\{(x^{(1)}, y^{(1)}), \dots, (x^{(m)}, y^{(m)})\}\).

Definition 3.1 (Single-Sample Mean Squared Error & Regularized Cost) For a single training example \((x, y)\), the half squared Euclidean error is defined as: \[ J(W, b; x, y) = \frac{1}{2} \| h_{W, b}(x) - y \|^2 = \frac{1}{2} \sum_{k=1}^{s_{n_l}} \left( a_k^{(n_l)} - y_k \right)^2 \] The overall regularized cost function averaged over all \(m\) training examples is: \[ J(W, b) = \left[ \frac{1}{m} \sum_{i=1}^m J\left(W, b; x^{(i)}, y^{(i)}\right) \right] + \frac{\lambda}{2} \sum_{l=1}^{n_l - 1} \sum_{i=1}^{s_l} \sum_{j=1}^{s_{l+1}} \left( W_{ji}^{(l)} \right)^2 \]
Slide Handwritten Annotation: Penalizing Weights vs. Biases (\(W, \cancel{b}\)) Notice that the \(L_2\) regularization penalty applies strictly to the multiplicative weights \(W_{ji}^{(l)}\) and NEVER to the additive bias terms \(b_i^{(l)}\) (penalizing \(W\) while explicitly excluding bias terms \(b\): \(W, \cancel{b}\)).
Regularizing the bias would penalize the network simply for shifting the baseline activation threshold away from zero, introducing undesirable shrinkage bias without improving capacity control.
Definition 3.2 (Multi-Class Cross-Entropy Cost Function) When the network output units represent Bernoulli probabilities (for multiple independent binary labels) or categorical distributions, the general multi-class cross-entropy objective is: \[ J(\Theta) = -\frac{1}{m} \sum_{i=1}^m \sum_{k=1}^K \left[ y_k^{(i)} \ln\left( (h_\Theta(x^{(i)}))_k \right) + (1 - y_k^{(i)}) \ln\left( 1 - (h_\Theta(x^{(i)}))_k \right) \right] + \frac{\lambda}{2m} \sum_{l=1}^{n_l - 1} \sum_{i=1}^{s_l} \sum_{j=1}^{s_{l+1}} \left( \Theta_{ji}^{(l)} \right)^2 \] where \(j\) sums from \(1\) to \(s_{l+1}\) and \(i\) sums from \(1\) to \(s_l\) (explicitly excluding the bias weight column \(\Theta_{j0}^{(l)}\)).

4. The Credit Assignment Problem & The Error Vector \(\delta^{(l)}\)

In linear models, computing the parameter gradient \(\nabla_\theta J(\theta)\) is straightforward because each parameter connects directly to the final output. In a deep network with \(L = n_l\) layers, an error committed at the output layer is the culmination of hundreds of thousands of interacting intermediate decisions made throughout the earlier layers.

The Credit Assignment Problem asks: How much responsibility (credit or blame) should be attributed to each individual synapse \(\Theta_{ji}^{(l)}\) in hidden layer \(l\) for the final output error?

Definition 4.1 (The Error Term \(\delta_i^{(l)}\)) For a given training example \((x, y)\), define the error term \(\delta_i^{(l)}\) of unit \(i\) in layer \(l\) as the sensitivity of the loss with respect to that unit's pre-activation net input \(z_i^{(l)}\): \[ \delta_i^{(l)} \equiv \frac{\partial}{\partial z_i^{(l)}} J(W, b; x, y) \] In vector notation for layer \(l\): \[ \delta^{(l)} \equiv \nabla_{z^{(l)}} J(W, b; x, y) = \begin{bmatrix} \delta_1^{(l)} \\ \delta_2^{(l)} \\ \vdots \\ \delta_{s_l}^{(l)} \end{bmatrix} \in \mathbb{R}^{s_l} \]

Once the error terms \(\delta^{(l)}\) are known for all layers, the partial derivatives with respect to the synaptic weights \(\Theta^{(l)}\) and biases \(b^{(l)}\) collapse to simple outer products of forward activations and backward errors!

5. Rigorous Step-by-Step Chain Rule Derivation of Backpropagation

We now derive the backpropagation equations step-by-step using the multivariate chain rule, mirroring the exact presentation in the lecture slides.

Step 1: Forward Propagation Recurrence Given an input vector \(x\), we initialize \(a^{(1)} = x\). For each layer \(l = 1, 2, \dots, n_l - 1\): \[ z^{(l+1)} = W^{(l)} a^{(l)} + b^{(l)} \quad \Longleftrightarrow \quad z_i^{(l+1)} = \sum_{j=1}^{s_l} W_{ij}^{(l)} a_j^{(l)} + b_i^{(l)} \] \[ a^{(l+1)} = f\left( z^{(l+1)} \right) \quad \Longleftrightarrow \quad a_i^{(l+1)} = f\left( z_i^{(l+1)} \right) \] where \(f(\cdot)\) is the element-wise activation function (e.g., sigmoid \(g\)).
Step 2: Output Layer Error (\(l = n_l\)) Let the loss be the single-sample MSE: \(J = \frac{1}{2} \sum_{k=1}^{s_{n_l}} (a_k^{(n_l)} - y_k)^2\). Applying the univariate chain rule to the pre-activation \(z_i^{(n_l)}\): \[ \delta_i^{(n_l)} = \frac{\partial J}{\partial z_i^{(n_l)}} = \frac{\partial J}{\partial a_i^{(n_l)}} \cdot \frac{\partial a_i^{(n_l)}}{\partial z_i^{(n_l)}} \] Differentiating each term: \[ \frac{\partial J}{\partial a_i^{(n_l)}} = (a_i^{(n_l)} - y_i) = -(y_i - a_i^{(n_l)}) \] \[ \frac{\partial a_i^{(n_l)}}{\partial z_i^{(n_l)}} = f'\left( z_i^{(n_l)} \right) \] Multiplying gives the fundamental output error formula: \[ \delta_i^{(n_l)} = -(y_i - a_i^{(n_l)}) \cdot f'\left( z_i^{(n_l)} \right) = \left(a_i^{(n_l)} - y_i\right) \cdot f'\left( z_i^{(n_l)} \right) \]
Case of Sigmoid Derivative: For the logistic sigmoid function \(g(z) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-z}}\), its derivative is \(g'(z) = g(z)(1 - g(z)) = a(1 - a)\). Therefore: \[ \delta_i^{(n_l)} = (a_i^{(n_l)} - y_i) \cdot a_i^{(n_l)} (1 - a_i^{(n_l)}) \] Note: For cross-entropy loss with sigmoid/softmax activations, the denominator of the loss gradient exactly cancels the derivative term \(g'(z)\), yielding simply \(\delta_i^{(n_l)} = a_i^{(n_l)} - y_i\).
Step 3: Hidden Layer Error Recurrence (\(l = n_l - 1, \dots, 2\)) Consider a hidden unit \(i\) in layer \(l\). Its pre-activation \(z_i^{(l)}\) affects the cost function only through its activation \(a_i^{(l)}\), which is subsequently fed forward to all units \(j = 1, \dots, s_{l+1}\) in the next layer \(l+1\).

By the multivariate chain rule: \[ \delta_i^{(l)} = \frac{\partial J}{\partial z_i^{(l)}} = \sum_{j=1}^{s_{l+1}} \frac{\partial J}{\partial z_j^{(l+1)}} \cdot \frac{\partial z_j^{(l+1)}}{\partial a_i^{(l)}} \cdot \frac{\partial a_i^{(l)}}{\partial z_i^{(l)}} \] Examine each constituent partial derivative:
  1. By definition of the layer \(l+1\) error: \[ \frac{\partial J}{\partial z_j^{(l+1)}} = \delta_j^{(l+1)} \]
  2. Since \(z_j^{(l+1)} = \sum_{k=1}^{s_l} W_{jk}^{(l)} a_k^{(l)} + b_j^{(l)}\), differentiating with respect to \(a_i^{(l)}\) yields: \[ \frac{\partial z_j^{(l+1)}}{\partial a_i^{(l)}} = W_{ji}^{(l)} \]
  3. Since \(a_i^{(l)} = f(z_i^{(l)})\), differentiating with respect to \(z_i^{(l)}\) yields: \[ \frac{\partial a_i^{(l)}}{\partial z_i^{(l)}} = f'\left( z_i^{(l)} \right) \]
Substituting these three terms back into the summation: \[ \delta_i^{(l)} = \left( \sum_{j=1}^{s_{l+1}} W_{ji}^{(l)} \delta_j^{(l+1)} \right) f'\left( z_i^{(l)} \right) \]

Interpretation: The error of unit \(i\) in layer \(l\) is the weighted combination of the errors \(\delta_j^{(l+1)}\) from the layer above, scaled by the incoming synapse weights \(W_{ji}^{(l)}\), and modulated by the local slope of the activation function \(f'(z_i^{(l)})\).

Step 4: Parameter Partial Derivatives (Synaptic Weights & Biases) With \(\delta_i^{(l+1)}\) computed, we evaluate the gradient of the loss with respect to individual weights \(W_{ij}^{(l)}\) and biases \(b_i^{(l)}\).

For synaptic weights \(W_{ij}^{(l)}\): \[ \frac{\partial J}{\partial W_{ij}^{(l)}} = \frac{\partial J}{\partial z_i^{(l+1)}} \cdot \frac{\partial z_i^{(l+1)}}{\partial W_{ij}^{(l)}} \] Since \(z_i^{(l+1)} = \sum_{k=1}^{s_l} W_{ik}^{(l)} a_k^{(l)} + b_i^{(l)}\), we have \(\frac{\partial z_i^{(l+1)}}{\partial W_{ij}^{(l)}} = a_j^{(l)}\). Therefore: \[ \frac{\partial J}{\partial W_{ij}^{(l)}} = \delta_i^{(l+1)} \cdot a_j^{(l)} = a_j^{(l)} \delta_i^{(l+1)} \]
For biases \(b_i^{(l)}\): \[ \frac{\partial J}{\partial b_i^{(l)}} = \frac{\partial J}{\partial z_i^{(l+1)}} \cdot \frac{\partial z_i^{(l+1)}}{\partial b_i^{(l)}} = \delta_i^{(l+1)} \cdot 1 = \delta_i^{(l+1)} \]

6. Vectorized Backpropagation & The Batch Gradient Descent Algorithm

Translating scalar element-wise equations into matrix operations allows deep learning frameworks to execute gradient computations at maximum hardware throughput on vectorized CPU/GPU matrix multiplication units.

Theorem 6.1 (The Vectorized Backpropagation Equations) Let \(\odot\) denote the Hadamard (element-wise) product.
  1. Output Layer Error Vector: \[ \delta^{(n_l)} = -(y - a^{(n_l)}) \odot f'\left( z^{(n_l)} \right) \in \mathbb{R}^{s_{n_l}} \]
  2. Hidden Layer Error Recurrence (\(l = n_l - 1, \dots, 2\)): \[ \delta^{(l)} = \left( \left( W^{(l)} \right)^T \delta^{(l+1)} \right) \odot f'\left( z^{(l)} \right) \in \mathbb{R}^{s_l} \]
  3. Weight Gradient Outer Product: \[ \nabla_{W^{(l)}} J(W, b; x, y) = \delta^{(l+1)} \left( a^{(l)} \right)^T \in \mathbb{R}^{s_{l+1} \times s_l} \]
  4. Bias Gradient Vector: \[ \nabla_{b^{(l)}} J(W, b; x, y) = \delta^{(l+1)} \in \mathbb{R}^{s_{l+1}} \]

Batch Gradient Descent with Backpropagation

For a full dataset of \(m\) training examples, the training algorithm accumulates gradients in memory buffers \(\Delta^{(l)}\):

Algorithm 6.1 (Batch Gradient Descent in Deep Networks)
  1. Initialize Accumulators: Set \(\Delta_{ij}^{(l)} := 0\) and \(\Delta_i^{(l)} := 0\) for all \(l, i, j\).
  2. Iterate Over Training Set: For each training example \(k = 1, \dots, m\):
    1. Perform forward propagation to compute all activations \(a^{(l)}\) and net inputs \(z^{(l)}\) for \(l = 2, \dots, n_l\).
    2. Evaluate the output error \(\delta^{(n_l)} = -(y^{(k)} - a^{(n_l)}) \odot f'(z^{(n_l)})\).
    3. Backpropagate errors \(\delta^{(l)} = ((W^{(l)})^T \delta^{(l+1)}) \odot f'(z^{(l)})\) for \(l = n_l - 1, \dots, 2\).
    4. Accumulate gradients: \[ \Delta_{ij}^{(l)} := \Delta_{ij}^{(l)} + a_j^{(l)} \delta_i^{(l+1)} \] \[ \Delta_i^{(l)} := \Delta_i^{(l)} + \delta_i^{(l+1)} \]
  3. Compute Regularized Average Derivatives: \[ D_{ij}^{(l)} = \begin{cases} \frac{1}{m} \Delta_{ij}^{(l)} + \lambda W_{ij}^{(l)} & \text{if } j \ge 1 \quad (\text{penalized weight synapses}) \\ \frac{1}{m} \Delta_{ij}^{(l)} & \text{if } j = 0 \quad (\text{unpenalized bias terms}) \end{cases} \]
  4. Update Network Parameters: Apply gradient descent with learning rate \(\alpha > 0\): \[ W^{(l)} := W^{(l)} - \alpha D^{(l)} \]

7. Symmetry Breaking, Random Initialization & Xavier/He Variance Scaling

In linear regression and logistic regression, parameter initialization is trivial: setting all weights to zero (\(\theta = 0\)) is completely standard because the loss surface is strictly convex. In deep neural networks, however, zero initialization is a catastrophic failure mode.

The Symmetry Problem Suppose we initialize all weights to zero: \(W_{ij}^{(l)} = 0\) for all \(i, j, l\).

During Forward Propagation: For any input \(x\), the pre-activation of every hidden unit in layer 2 is identical: \[ z_i^{(2)} = \sum_{j} 0 \cdot x_j + 0 = 0 \implies a_1^{(2)} = a_2^{(2)} = \dots = a_{s_2}^{(2)} = f(0) \] During Backpropagation: Because all outgoing weights from layer 2 are identical, every hidden unit receives the exact same backpropagated error signal: \[ \delta_1^{(2)} = \delta_2^{(2)} = \dots = \delta_{s_2}^{(2)} \] Consequently, the gradient update for every weight in the layer is identical: \[ \frac{\partial J}{\partial W_{i1}^{(1)}} = \frac{\partial J}{\partial W_{i2}^{(1)}} = \dots \] On every iteration of gradient descent, all hidden neurons remain mathematically identical copies of each other. The entire hidden layer of \(s_2\) neurons collapses functionally to a single neuron! The network can never break symmetry to learn diverse feature detectors.
Symmetry Breaking via Random Initialization To break symmetry, parameters must be initialized randomly from a continuous zero-mean distribution: \[ W_{ij}^{(l)} \sim \text{Uniform}[-\epsilon, +\epsilon] \quad \text{or} \quad W_{ij}^{(l)} \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2) \] where \(\epsilon\) is a small positive scalar (e.g., \(\epsilon = 0.12\)).

Modern Variance Scaling: Xavier (Glorot) and He (Kaiming) Initialization

As highlighted in slide 45, if the initial variance \(\sigma^2\) is chosen too small, activations and backpropagated gradients shrink exponentially with depth (vanishing gradient). If \(\sigma^2\) is chosen too large, pre-activations explode into saturation regions where \(f'(z) \approx 0\) (saturation paralysis).

Variance Preservation Principle To preserve the variance of signals across layers during both the forward pass and backward pass:
  • Xavier / Glorot Initialization (for Sigmoid and Tanh activations): \[ W_{ij}^{(l)} \sim \mathcal{N}\left(0, \; \frac{2}{n_{\text{in}} + n_{\text{out}}}\right) = \mathcal{N}\left(0, \; \frac{2}{s_l + s_{l+1}}\right) \]
  • He / Kaiming Initialization (for ReLU activations): Because ReLU zeros out half of the pre-activations on average (\(\mathbb{E}[\text{ReLU}(z)^2] = \frac{1}{2}\text{Var}(z)\)), the variance must be doubled to compensate: \[ W_{ij}^{(l)} \sim \mathcal{N}\left(0, \; \frac{2}{n_{\text{in}}}\right) = \mathcal{N}\left(0, \; \frac{2}{s_l}\right) \]
Biases can be safely initialized to zeros: \(b^{(l)} = 0\).

8. Practical Diagnostics: Gradient Checking & The Non-Convex Optimization Landscape

Because backpropagation involves complex matrix dimensions, transposes, and index book-keeping, an implementation with subtle bugs often appears to run without crashing, yet achieves poor final accuracy or converges at an agonizingly slow pace.

Numerical Gradient Checking To verify analytical backpropagation, we approximate the gradient numerically using the symmetric two-sided finite difference quotient.

Let all network parameters be unrolled into a single long parameter vector \(\theta \in \mathbb{R}^P\). For each individual parameter \(\theta_i\): \[ \frac{\partial J}{\partial \theta_i} \approx \frac{J(\theta_1, \dots, \theta_i + \epsilon, \dots, \theta_P) - J(\theta_1, \dots, \theta_i - \epsilon, \dots, \theta_P)}{2\epsilon} \] where \(\epsilon \approx 10^{-4}\) (or \(10^{-7}\) in double precision).

Verification Metric: Compute the relative Euclidean distance between the analytical gradient vector \(d_{\text{analytic}}\) and numerical approximation \(d_{\text{approx}}\): \[ \text{Relative Difference} = \frac{\| d_{\text{analytic}} - d_{\text{approx}} \|_2}{\| d_{\text{analytic}} \|_2 + \| d_{\text{approx}} \|_2} \]
  • If the relative difference is \(\le 10^{-7}\): The backpropagation implementation is provably correct!
  • If the relative difference is \(\approx 10^{-4}\): Likely suspicious; check for regularization terms or indexing offsets.
  • If the relative difference is \(\ge 10^{-2}\): Severe bug in gradient derivation or code logic.
Diagnostic Rule: Numerical gradient checking is computationally expensive (\(2P\) forward passes). It must be run only on tiny synthetic datasets and small architectures for testing, and turned off completely during actual training!

The Non-Convex Optimization Landscape

A central theoretical distinction is the fundamental geometric nature of the neural network loss surface:

Non-Convexity & Local Optima In Linear Regression and Logistic Regression, the objective function \(J(\theta)\) is strictly convex (its Hessian \(\nabla^2 J\) is positive semi-definite everywhere). Consequently, every local minimum is a global minimum, and gradient descent is guaranteed to find the unique global optimal parameter vector.

In Deep Neural Networks, the composition of non-linear activations across multiple layers renders the objective function \(J(\Theta)\) fundamentally non-convex: \[ J(\Theta) \text{ is non-convex: } \quad \exists \text{ multiple local minima, saddle points, and flat plateaus} \] Optimization Note: Due to non-convexity, gradient-based optimization is sensitive to initialization and may settle in suboptimal local minima.

Sources of Non-Convexity:
  1. Permutation Invariance (Symmetry): In any hidden layer with \(s_l\) neurons, permuting the indices of the neurons and their corresponding incoming and outgoing weights leaves the input-output function completely unchanged. For a layer with \(s_l\) neurons, there exist \(s_l!\) distinct parameter configurations that produce identical predictions. The parameter space thus contains at least \(\prod_{l=2}^{n_l-1} s_l!\) equivalent global minima!
  2. Saddle Points & Flat Plateaus: In high-dimensional optimization, local minima where all eigenvalues of the Hessian are positive are actually rare compared to saddle points (where the gradient vanishes, but some directions curve upward while others curve downward).
Despite non-convexity, modern empirical research and over-parameterization theory show that stochastic gradient descent (SGD), momentum, and adaptive optimizers (Adam, RMSProp) routinely navigate high-dimensional loss landscapes to discover solutions that generalize exceptionally well.
